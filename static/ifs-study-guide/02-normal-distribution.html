01. Intuition & Concept Contrast: Population vs Sample Mean

The Normal distribution is the foundation of parametric inference. When analyzing single observations ($X$), dispersion is governed by $\sigma$. When analyzing sample means ($\bar{X}$), dispersion shrinks by $\sqrt{n}$ due to the Central Limit Theorem.

Individual Observation · Raw Variate

$X \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)$

Measures single units (e.g., one student's mark, one household's water usage). Standardized using the population standard deviation:

$$\begin{aligned} Z = \frac{X - \mu}{\sigma} \end{aligned}$$
Sample Mean · Sampling Distribution

$\bar{X} \sim \mathcal{N}\left(\mu, \frac{\sigma^2}{n}\right)$

Measures averages across sample size $n \ge 30$. By CLT, dispersion contracts to the Standard Error $\sigma_{\bar{X}} = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}$:

$$\begin{aligned} Z = \frac{\bar{X} - \mu}{\sigma / \sqrt{n}} \end{aligned}$$
The Empirical 68–95–99.7 Rule:
• $P(\mu \pm 1\sigma) \approx \mathbf{68.27\%}$ of all observations fall within 1 SD.
• $P(\mu \pm 2\sigma) \approx \mathbf{95.45\%}$ fall within 2 SD (approximately $Z = \pm 1.96$ for $95.0\%$).
• $P(\mu \pm 3\sigma) \approx \mathbf{99.73\%}$ fall within 3 SD (industrial Six Sigma boundary).

02. Mathematical Formulation & 7 Key Properties

A continuous random variable $X$ is normally distributed with mean $\mu \in (-\infty, \infty)$ and variance $\sigma^2 > 0$ if its Probability Density Function (PDF) is:

$$\begin{aligned} f(x) = \frac{1}{\sigma \sqrt{2\pi}} \exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{x - \mu}{\sigma}\right)^2\right), \quad -\infty < x < \infty \end{aligned}$$ Standard Normal Variate: $Z = \frac{X - \mu}{\sigma} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$ with PDF $\phi(z) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-z^2/2}$

7 Compulsory Theoretical Properties (University Rubric)

  1. Perfect Symmetry: Bell-shaped curve perfectly symmetric around the mean $x = \mu$.
  2. Equivalence of Averages: $\text{Mean} = \text{Median} = \text{Mode} = \mu$.
  3. Total Area Under Curve: $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(x)\,dx = 1.00$ ($50\%$ on left of $\mu$, $50\%$ on right).
  4. Points of Inflection: The curvature shifts from concave down to concave up at exactly $x = \mu \pm \sigma$.
  5. Zero Skewness ($\beta_1 = 0$): Moment ratio $\beta_1 = \frac{\mu_3^2}{\mu_2^3} = 0 \implies \gamma_1 = 0$.
  6. Mesokurtic Peak ($\beta_2 = 3$): Kurtosis ratio $\beta_2 = \frac{\mu_4}{\mu_2^2} = 3 \implies \text{Excess kurtosis } \gamma_2 = 0$.
  7. Asymptotic Tails: The curve approaches the horizontal axis asymptotically as $x \to \pm\infty$ but never touches it.

03. Reading the Standard Normal $Z$-Table (The 4 Rules)

Standard normal tables tabulate the cumulative distribution function $\Phi(z) = P(Z \le z)$ for non-negative values of $z$. Use these 4 mathematical rules:

Desired Area / Probability Mathematical Rule Example ($z = 1.50$)
Left-Tail: $P(Z \le z)$ Direct lookup: $\Phi(z)$ $P(Z \le 1.50) = 0.9332$
Right-Tail: $P(Z > z)$ Complement rule: $1 - \Phi(z)$ $P(Z > 1.50) = 1 - 0.9332 = \mathbf{0.0668}$
Negative $Z$: $P(Z \le -z)$ Symmetry rule: $1 - \Phi(z)$ $P(Z \le -1.50) = 1 - 0.9332 = \mathbf{0.0668}$
Between: $P(z_1 \le Z \le z_2)$ Difference: $\Phi(z_2) - \Phi(z_1)$ $P(-1 \le Z \le 1.5) = 0.9332 - 0.1587 = \mathbf{0.7745}$
Key Critical Z-Values to Memorize:
• $90\%$ Confidence (Two-tailed): $Z_{\alpha/2} = \mathbf{\pm 1.645}$
• $95\%$ Confidence (Two-tailed): $Z_{\alpha/2} = \mathbf{\pm 1.960}$
• $99\%$ Confidence (Two-tailed): $Z_{\alpha/2} = \mathbf{\pm 2.576}$

04. Central Limit Theorem (CLT) Deep Dive

The Central Limit Theorem explains why the Normal distribution dominates statistical science. It connects arbitrary parent populations to Gaussian inference.

Formal CLT Theorem Statement

Let $X_1, X_2, \dots, X_n$ be a sequence of independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) random variables sampled from any population distribution with finite mean $\mu$ and finite variance $\sigma^2$. As sample size $n \to \infty$ (in practice, $n \ge 30$):

$$\begin{aligned} \bar{X} \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}\left(\mu, \frac{\sigma^2}{n}\right) \implies Z = \frac{\bar{X} - \mu}{\sigma / \sqrt{n}} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1) \end{aligned}$$
Application 1 · Sample Mean Bounds

Sample Error Bound

To assert that the sample mean does not differ from population mean $\mu$ by more than margin $E$ ($P(|\bar{X} - \mu| \le E)$):

$$\begin{aligned} Z = \frac{E}{\sigma / \sqrt{n}} \end{aligned}$$
Application 2 · Binomial Approximation

Continuity Correction ($\pm 0.5$)

When approximating discrete Binomial counts ($n \ge 30, np \ge 5$): $\mu = np, \sigma = \sqrt{npq}$.

$$\begin{aligned} P(a \le X \le b) \approx P(a - 0.5 \le X \le b + 0.5) \end{aligned}$$

05. 6 Deadly Exam Traps & Common Student Mistakes

Trap 1 · Forgetting $\sqrt{n}$ on Sample Means

Dividing by $\sigma$ instead of $\sigma / \sqrt{n}$

If the question says "a sample of size 100" and asks about the sample mean $\bar{X}$, you MUST divide by the standard error $\sigma/\sqrt{n}$, not $\sigma$!

Trap 2 · Variance vs Standard Deviation

Using $\sigma^2 = 400$ directly as 400

If the problem gives "variance 400", take the square root immediately: $\sigma = \sqrt{400} = 20$. Dividing by 400 destroys the entire derivation.

Trap 3 · Probability vs Student Count

Leaving the answer as $0.8185$ instead of 82 students

When asked for "the number of students" out of $N = 100$, multiply: $\text{Count} = N \times P = 100 \times 0.8185 \approx \mathbf{82 \text{ students}}$.

Trap 4 · Reversed Tail in "More Than"

Reporting $\Phi(z)$ instead of $1 - \Phi(z)$

For $P(X > x)$, the area is in the upper right tail. Always subtract: $P(X > x) = 1 - \Phi(z)$.

Trap 5 · Missing Continuity Correction

Neglecting $\pm 0.5$ in Discrete Coin / Count CLT

For discrete integer intervals like $120 \le X \le 140$, always expand to $119.5 \le X \le 140.5$ to capture the full integer bin.

Trap 6 · Symmetry Sign Confusion

Evaluating $\Phi(-1.43)$ as $\Phi(1.43)$

Standard tables only show positive $z$. By symmetry: $\Phi(-z) = 1 - \Phi(z) = 1 - 0.9236 = \mathbf{0.0764}$.

Question 1: Normal Distribution Properties & Class Grade Modeling (10 Marks)

Question Statement (Verbatim):
"Explain the normal distribution in detail with the help of mark or grade distribution of the class with some data. Discuss its properties, significance in statistics, and applications in real-life data analysis. (10 Marks)"

View Complete Model Solution & Grade Distribution Walkthrough

Part 1: Academic Example (Class Grade Distribution Modeling)

Consider an exam cohort of $N = 500$ students where marks follow $X \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu = 70, \sigma = 10)$:

  • Grade A ($X \ge 85$): $$\begin{aligned} Z &= \frac{85 - 70}{10} = +1.50 \\[3pt] P(X \ge 85) &= 1 - \Phi(1.50) = 1 - 0.9332 = \mathbf{0.0668 \ (6.68\%)} \\[3pt] \text{Students} &= 500 \times 0.0668 \approx \mathbf{33 \text{ students}} \end{aligned}$$
  • Grade B ($70 \le X < 85$): $$\begin{aligned} P(0 \le Z < 1.50) &= \Phi(1.50) - 0.5000 = 0.9332 - 0.5000 = \mathbf{0.4332 \ (43.32\%)} \\[3pt] \text{Students} &= 500 \times 0.4332 \approx \mathbf{217 \text{ students}} \end{aligned}$$
  • Grade C ($55 \le X < 70$): $$\begin{aligned} Z &= \frac{55 - 70}{10} = -1.50 \implies P(-1.50 \le Z < 0) = \mathbf{0.4332} \\[3pt] \text{Students} &= 500 \times 0.4332 \approx \mathbf{217 \text{ students}} \end{aligned}$$
  • Grade F ($X < 55$): $$\begin{aligned} P(X < 55) &= \Phi(-1.50) = 1 - 0.9332 = \mathbf{0.0668 \ (6.68\%)} \\[3pt] \text{Students} &= 500 \times 0.0668 \approx \mathbf{33 \text{ students}} \end{aligned}$$
Significance in Statistics: Serves as the central limiting model of empirical data, enables standardization via $Z$-scores, and provides the baseline for inferential hypothesis testing ($Z, t, F, \chi^2$).

Question 2: Student Marks Estimation & Practical Applications (20 Marks)

Question Statement (Verbatim):
"The marks of 100 students in an exam are found to be normally distributed with mean 70 and S.D. 5. Estimate the number of students whose marks will be: (i) between 60 and 75, (ii) more than 75 marks, (iii) less than 65 marks. Also explain the uses of normal distribution briefly. (20 Marks)"

View Complete Step-by-Step Solution & 5 Practical Uses

Given Parameters: Total students $N = 100$; Distribution $X \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu = 70, \sigma = 5)$. Standardized transformation: $Z = \frac{X - 70}{5}$.

(i) Marks Between 60 and 75 (4 Marks)

$$\begin{aligned} z_1 &= \frac{60 - 70}{5} = \mathbf{-2.0}, \quad z_2 = \frac{75 - 70}{5} = \mathbf{+1.0} \\[4pt] P(60 \le X \le 75) &= \Phi(1.0) - \Phi(-2.0) = 0.8413 - (1 - 0.9772) \\[4pt] &= 0.8413 - 0.0228 = \mathbf{0.8185 \ (81.85\%)} \\[4pt] \text{Expected Count} &= 100 \times 0.8185 = \mathbf{81.85 \approx 82 \text{ students}} \end{aligned}$$

(ii) Marks More Than 75 (4 Marks)

$$\begin{aligned} z &= \frac{75 - 70}{5} = \mathbf{+1.0} \\[4pt] P(X > 75) &= 1 - \Phi(1.0) = 1 - 0.8413 = \mathbf{0.1587 \ (15.87\%)} \\[4pt] \text{Expected Count} &= 100 \times 0.1587 = \mathbf{15.87 \approx 16 \text{ students}} \end{aligned}$$

(iii) Marks Less Than 65 (4 Marks)

$$\begin{aligned} z &= \frac{65 - 70}{5} = \mathbf{-1.0} \\[4pt] P(X < 65) &= \Phi(-1.0) = 1 - \Phi(1.0) = 1 - 0.8413 = \mathbf{0.1587 \ (15.87\%)} \\[4pt] \text{Expected Count} &= 100 \times 0.1587 = \mathbf{15.87 \approx 16 \text{ students}} \end{aligned}$$

Part 2: 5 Practical Uses of Normal Distribution (8 Marks)

  1. Parametric Inference: Serves as the mathematical basis for $Z$-tests, $t$-tests, ANOVA, and linear regression residual testing.
  2. Vehicle for Central Limit Theorem: Ensures sample averages converge to normality regardless of the underlying population shape.
  3. Statistical Quality Control (Six Sigma): Establishes industrial control charts with upper/lower action limits at $\mu \pm 3\sigma$ ($99.73\%$ tolerance).
  4. Grading on a Curve & Psychometrics: Scales exam grades, standardized SAT/GRE percentiles, and IQ metrics (mean 100, SD 15).
  5. Financial Risk Modeling: Calculates portfolio Value-at-Risk (VaR) under log-normal asset return assumptions.

Question 3: CLT Estimation of Sample Mean Deviation (8 Marks)

Question Statement (Verbatim):
"A random sample of size 100 is taken from a population whose mean is 60 and variance 400. Estimate using central limit theorem, with what probability can we assert that mean of the sample will not differ from $\mu = 60$ by more than 4. (8 Marks)"

View Complete Step-by-Step Solution

1. Identify Population & Sample Parameters:

  • Sample size: $n = 100$
  • Population mean: $\mu = 60$
  • Population variance: $\sigma^2 = 400 \implies \sigma = \sqrt{400} = \mathbf{20}$

2. Apply Central Limit Theorem:

Since $n = 100 \ge 30$, by CLT the sample mean $\bar{X}$ is normally distributed:

$$\begin{aligned} \bar{X} \sim \mathcal{N}\left(\mu, \sigma_{\bar{X}}^2\right), \quad \text{where } \sigma_{\bar{X}} = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}} = \frac{20}{\sqrt{100}} = \frac{20}{10} = \mathbf{2.0} \end{aligned}$$

3. Formulate Probability Statement:

We want the probability that $\bar{X}$ does not differ from $60$ by more than $4$:

$$\begin{aligned} P(|\bar{X} - 60| \le 4) &= P(60 - 4 \le \bar{X} \le 60 + 4) \\[4pt] &= P(56 \le \bar{X} \le 64) \end{aligned}$$

4. Standardize to $Z$-Scores:

$$\begin{aligned} z_1 &= \frac{56 - 60}{2.0} = \frac{-4}{2.0} = \mathbf{-2.0} \\[4pt] z_2 &= \frac{64 - 60}{2.0} = \frac{+4}{2.0} = \mathbf{+2.0} \end{aligned}$$

5. Compute Probability from $Z$-Table:

$$\begin{aligned} P(-2.0 \le Z \le +2.0) &= \Phi(2.0) - \Phi(-2.0) \\[4pt] &= 0.9772 - (1 - 0.9772) \\[4pt] &= 0.9772 - 0.0228 = \mathbf{0.9544 \ (95.44\%)} \end{aligned}$$
Conclusion: Using the Central Limit Theorem, we can assert with 95.44% probability that the sample mean will lie within 4 units of the true population mean.

Question 4: CLT Normal Approximation for Coin Tossing (8 Marks)

Question Statement (Verbatim):
"A fair coin is tossed 250 times. Find the probability that heads will appear between 120 and 140 times using CLT. (8 Marks)"

View Complete Step-by-Step Solution & Continuity Correction

1. Establish Parameters:

Let $X$ denote the number of heads in $n = 250$ independent coin tosses ($p = 0.5, q = 0.5$):

$$\begin{aligned} \text{Mean: } \mu &= np = 250 \times 0.5 = \mathbf{125} \\[3pt] \text{Variance: } \sigma^2 &= npq = 250 \times 0.5 \times 0.5 = \mathbf{62.5} \\[3pt] \text{Standard Deviation: } \sigma &= \sqrt{62.5} \approx \mathbf{7.9057} \end{aligned}$$

2. Continuity Correction for $120 \le X \le 140$:

Because Binomial counts are discrete integers, apply continuity correction ($\pm 0.5$):

$$\begin{aligned} P(120 \le X \le 140) \approx P(119.5 \le X \le 140.5) \end{aligned}$$

3. Standardize to $Z$-Scores:

$$\begin{aligned} z_1 &= \frac{119.5 - 125}{7.9057} = \frac{-5.5}{7.9057} \approx \mathbf{-0.70} \\[4pt] z_2 &= \frac{140.5 - 125}{7.9057} = \frac{15.5}{7.9057} \approx \mathbf{+1.96} \end{aligned}$$

4. Area Computation:

$$\begin{aligned} P(-0.70 \le Z \le 1.96) &= \Phi(1.96) - \Phi(-0.70) \\[4pt] &= 0.9750 - (1 - 0.7580) \\[4pt] &= 0.9750 - 0.2420 = \mathbf{0.7330 \ (73.30\%)} \end{aligned}$$
Examiner's Note: Without continuity correction, $z_1 = \frac{120-125}{7.9057} = -0.63$ and $z_2 = \frac{140-125}{7.9057} = 1.90 \implies P = \Phi(1.90) - \Phi(-0.63) = 0.9713 - 0.2643 = \mathbf{0.7070}$. State both to demonstrate comprehensive competence!

Question 5: Household Electricity Bill Normal Probability (6 Marks)

Question Statement (Verbatim):
"The monthly electricity bill of households in a city follows a normal distribution, with a mean of $120 and a variance of $49. What is the probability that a randomly selected household has a monthly bill less than $110? (6 Marks)"

View Complete Step-by-Step Solution

1. Identify Distribution:

Let $X$ denote the monthly electricity bill in dollars:

$$\begin{aligned} X \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu = 120, \sigma^2 = 49) \implies \sigma = \sqrt{49} = \mathbf{7} \end{aligned}$$

2. Standard Normal Transformation:

$$\begin{aligned} Z &= \frac{X - \mu}{\sigma} = \frac{110 - 120}{7} = \frac{-10}{7} \approx \mathbf{-1.43} \end{aligned}$$

3. Probability Computation via Symmetry:

$$\begin{aligned} P(X < 110) &= P(Z < -1.43) = \Phi(-1.43) \\[4pt] &= 1 - \Phi(1.43) \\[4pt] &= 1 - 0.9236 = \mathbf{0.0764 \ (7.64\%)} \end{aligned}$$
Conclusion: The probability that a randomly selected household's monthly electricity bill is less than $110 is 0.0764 (or 7.64%).

Question 6: Household Daily Water Consumption (5 Marks)

Question Statement:
"Household daily water consumption follows a normal distribution with mean 180 litres and variance 400 litres². What is the probability that a randomly selected household consumes more than 215 litres in a day? (Given: $P(Z \le 1.75) = 0.9599$) (5 Marks)"

View Complete Step-by-Step Solution

1. Given Parameters: $\mu = 180 \text{ L}, \; \sigma^2 = 400 \implies \sigma = \sqrt{400} = \mathbf{20 \text{ L}}$. Threshold $x = 215 \text{ L}$.

2. Compute $Z$-Score:

$$\begin{aligned} Z = \frac{X - \mu}{\sigma} = \frac{215 - 180}{20} = \frac{35}{20} = \mathbf{+1.75} \end{aligned}$$

3. Compute Right-Tail Probability:

$$\begin{aligned} P(X > 215) &= P(Z > 1.75) = 1 - P(Z \le 1.75) \\[4pt] &= 1 - 0.9599 = \mathbf{0.0401 \ (4.01\%)} \end{aligned}$$
Conclusion: The probability of consuming more than 215 litres is 0.0401 (or 4.01%).

Question 7: Corporate Salary Normal Distribution

Question Statement:
"Annual salaries in a corporation follow a normal distribution with mean $75,000 and variance $4,000,000. Find: (i) Probability a salary exceeds $78,000, (ii) Probability a salary is less than $73,000."

View Complete Step-by-Step Solution

1. Distribution Parameters: $\mu = 75,000, \; \sigma = \sqrt{4,000,000} = \mathbf{2,000}$. Transformation: $Z = \frac{X - 75000}{2000}$.

(i) Salary Exceeding $78,000 ($P(X > 78,000)$):

$$\begin{aligned} Z &= \frac{78,000 - 75,000}{2,000} = \frac{3,000}{2,000} = \mathbf{+1.50} \\[4pt] P(X > 78,000) &= 1 - \Phi(1.50) = 1 - 0.9332 = \mathbf{0.0668 \ (6.68\%)} \end{aligned}$$

(ii) Salary Less than $73,000 ($P(X < 73,000)$):

$$\begin{aligned} Z &= \frac{73,000 - 75,000}{2,000} = \frac{-2,000}{2,000} = \mathbf{-1.00} \\[4pt] P(X < 73,000) &= \Phi(-1.00) = 1 - \Phi(1.00) = 1 - 0.8413 = \mathbf{0.1587 \ (15.87\%)} \end{aligned}$$
Summary: Probability $> \$78\text{k} = \mathbf{6.68\%}$; Probability $< \$73\text{k} = \mathbf{15.87\%}$.

Question 1 — Standard Error Calculation

A population has standard deviation $\sigma = 20$. If a random sample of $n = 100$ is drawn, what is the standard error of the sample mean $\sigma_{\bar{X}}$?

Correct: $\sigma_{\bar{X}} = 2.00$.
$\sigma_{\bar{X}} = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}} = \frac{20}{\sqrt{100}} = \frac{20}{10} = 2.00$. This is the exact denominator required for the December 2024 CLT question.
Question 2 — Symmetry of Standard Normal Distribution

If $\Phi(1.43) = 0.9236$, what is the left-tail probability $P(Z < -1.43)$?

Correct: $0.0764$ ($7.64\%$).
By symmetry, the left tail below $-z$ equals the right tail above $+z$: $P(Z < -1.43) = 1 - \Phi(1.43) = 1 - 0.9236 = 0.0764$.
Question 3 — Kurtosis & Skewness of Normal Distribution

What are the theoretical values of skewness ($\beta_1$) and kurtosis ($\beta_2$) for any true normal distribution?

Correct: $\beta_1 = 0$, $\beta_2 = 3$.
A normal curve is mesokurtic ($\beta_2 = 3$ or excess kurtosis $\gamma_2 = \beta_2 - 3 = 0$) and perfectly symmetric ($\beta_1 = 0$).
Question 4 — Continuity Correction Application

When approximating a discrete count $X \in [120, 140]$ using a continuous Normal curve under CLT, what adjusted bounds should be standardized?

Correct: $[119.5, 140.5]$.
To encompass the full integer bins for 120 and 140 inclusive, subtract $0.5$ from the lower limit and add $0.5$ to the upper limit.

08. Exam Hall Answer Sheet Presentation Checklist

When solving Normal Distribution & CLT questions in the semester exam, follow this exact structure to guarantee maximum marks:

  1. State the Distribution & Parameters: State $X \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)$ or $\bar{X} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2/n)$. If variance $\sigma^2$ is given, write $\sigma = \sqrt{\sigma^2}$ explicitly.
  2. State the $Z$-Score Formula: Write $Z = \frac{X - \mu}{\sigma}$ (or $Z = \frac{\bar{X} - \mu}{\sigma/\sqrt{n}}$ for CLT sample averages).
  3. Transform Bounds: Calculate $z_1$ and $z_2$ to 2 decimal places.
  4. Write Probability Statement: Express $P(X \dots) = P(Z \dots) = \Phi(z_2) - \Phi(z_1)$.
  5. Substitute Table Values: Show the cumulative $\Phi(z)$ values.
  6. Multiply for Counts (if requested): If asked for student/unit counts, show $\text{Count} = N \times P$ and round appropriately.
  7. State Final Conclusion: Provide both percentage and plain-text interpretation.
