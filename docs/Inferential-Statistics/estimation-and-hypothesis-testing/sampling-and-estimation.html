Sampling and Estimation

Topic - In real-world data science, measuring an entire population is rarely feasible due to operational constraints. Instead, we use sampling distributions to estimate unknown population parameters. Point estimation produces a single best-guess statistic, while interval estimation constructs a confidence interval bounded by a margin of error.

1. Population vs. Sample & The Need for Sampling​

  • Population: The complete collection of all elements, individuals, or items under study (e.g., all manufactured batteries from a factory). Characteristics of a population are called parameters (mean μ\muμ, variance σ2\sigma^2σ2, proportion ppp).
  • Sample: A representative subset drawn from the population. Numerical summaries computed from a sample are called statistics (sample mean xˉ\bar{x}xˉ, sample variance s2s^2s2, sample proportion p^\hat{p}p^​).

The Practical Need to Sample​

  1. Destructive Testing: Evaluating battery lifespan or the tensile strength of an alloy exhausts the product. Testing the whole population leaves zero inventory to sell.
  2. Resource Constraints: Gathering data on large populations (such as all citizens in a country) is cost-prohibitive and computationally intractable.
  3. Efficiency: An appropriately sized, unbiased random sample provides precise, mathematically bounded estimates with minimal variance.

2. Sampling Distributions & The Central Limit Theorem (CLT)​

When drawing kkk independent random samples of size nnn from a population, the calculated sample statistics (xˉ1,xˉ2,…,xˉk)(\bar{x}_1, \bar{x}_2, \dots, \bar{x}_k)(xˉ1​,xˉ2​,…,xˉk​) will naturally fluctuate. This variation across repeated samples is called sampling variability.

The probability distribution of these sample means is the Sampling Distribution of the Mean.

The Central Limit Theorem Formulation​

The Central Limit Theorem (CLT)

Regardless of the underlying population distribution (whether Normal, Poisson, Binomial, or Uniform), as the sample size nnn becomes sufficiently large (n≥30n \ge 30n≥30), the sampling distribution of the sample mean Xˉ\bar{X}Xˉ approaches a Normal Distribution:

Xˉ∼N(μXˉ=μ,  σXˉ=σn)\bar{X} \sim \mathcal{N}\left( \mu_{\bar{X}} = \mu, \; \sigma_{\bar{X}} = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}} \right)Xˉ∼N(μXˉ​=μ,σXˉ​=n​σ​)

Where σXˉ=σn\sigma_{\bar{X}} = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}σXˉ​=n​σ​ is called the Standard Error of the Mean (SE).

Converting the sample mean to a standard normal variable:

Z=Xˉ−μσ/n∼N(0,1)Z = \frac{\bar{X} - \mu}{\sigma / \sqrt{n}} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)Z=σ/n​Xˉ−μ​∼N(0,1)

If the population standard deviation σ\sigmaσ is unknown, we substitute the sample standard deviation sss, yielding Z≈Xˉ−μs/nZ \approx \frac{\bar{X} - \mu}{s / \sqrt{n}}Z≈s/n​Xˉ−μ​ for large samples (n≥30n \ge 30n≥30).

Sampling Error​

The point estimate will differ from the true population parameter due to random chance. The absolute magnitude of this discrepancy is the Sampling Error:

Sampling Error=∣xˉ−μ∣\text{Sampling Error} = |\bar{x} - \mu|Sampling Error=∣xˉ−μ∣

Increasing the sample size nnn consistently drives down the standard error (σ/n\sigma / \sqrt{n}σ/n​), decreasing sampling error.

4. Interval Estimation & Confidence Intervals​

Because a point estimate rarely matches the true parameter exactly, Interval Estimation provides a range of plausible values [L,U][L, U][L,U] associated with a specified confidence level (1−α1 - \alpha1−α).

Derivation for Population Mean (μ\muμ)​

From the Central Limit Theorem, the probability that the standardized statistic ZZZ falls between two critical values −zα/2-z_{\alpha/2}−zα/2​ and zα/2z_{\alpha/2}zα/2​ is 1−α1 - \alpha1−α:

P(−zα/2≤Xˉ−μσ/n≤zα/2)=1−αP\left( -z_{\alpha/2} \le \frac{\bar{X} - \mu}{\sigma / \sqrt{n}} \le z_{\alpha/2} \right) = 1 - \alphaP(−zα/2​≤σ/n​Xˉ−μ​≤zα/2​)=1−α

Rearranging the inequality:

P(Xˉ−zα/2σn≤μ≤Xˉ+zα/2σn)=1−αP\left( \bar{X} - z_{\alpha/2}\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}} \le \mu \le \bar{X} + z_{\alpha/2}\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}} \right) = 1 - \alphaP(Xˉ−zα/2​n​σ​≤μ≤Xˉ+zα/2​n​σ​)=1−α

Thus, the 100(1−α)%100(1 - \alpha)\%100(1−α)% Confidence Interval is:

CI=Xˉ±E\text{CI} = \bar{X} \pm ECI=Xˉ±E

Where E=zα/2σnE = z_{\alpha/2}\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}E=zα/2​n​σ​ is the Margin of Error.

As the confidence level increases (e.g., from 90% to 99%), zα/2z_{\alpha/2}zα/2​ grows larger, making the interval wider. A higher degree of certainty requires a wider net.

5. Confidence Intervals for Proportions​

For categorical variables (such as defective items or churn status), the sampling distribution of the sample proportion p^=x/n\hat{p} = x/np^​=x/n follows an approximate normal distribution for large samples:

p^∼N(p,  p(1−p)n)\hat{p} \sim \mathcal{N}\left( p, \; \sqrt{\frac{p(1 - p)}{n}} \right)p^​∼N(p,np(1−p)​​)

The 100(1−α)%100(1 - \alpha)\%100(1−α)% Confidence Interval for Proportion is:

CIp=p^±zα/2p^(1−p^)n\text{CI}_p = \hat{p} \pm z_{\alpha/2} \sqrt{\frac{\hat{p}(1 - \hat{p})}{n}}CIp​=p^​±zα/2​np^​(1−p^​)​​

6. Determining Sample Size from Margin of Error​

When designing an experiment or survey, a primary goal is to determine how many observations nnn must be sampled to guarantee a maximum desired margin of error EEE:

E=zα/2σn  ⟹  n=zα/2⋅σE  ⟹  n=zα/22(σ2E2)E = z_{\alpha/2}\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}} \implies \sqrt{n} = \frac{z_{\alpha/2}\cdot \sigma}{E} \implies n = z_{\alpha/2}^2 \left( \frac{\sigma^2}{E^2} \right)E=zα/2​n​σ​⟹n​=Ezα/2​⋅σ​⟹n=zα/22​(E2σ2​)

7. Numerical Problem Walkthroughs​

Scenario A: Portfolio Point Estimate​

Problem: A financial firm manages N=50N = 50N=50 portfolios. A sample of n=13n = 13n=13 portfolios is audited, and x=8x = 8x=8 are found underperforming. Estimate the total number of underperforming portfolios in the firm.

  1. Sample Proportion: p^=xn=813≈0.6154\hat{p} = \frac{x}{n} = \frac{8}{13} \approx 0.6154p^​=nx​=138​≈0.6154
  2. Total Estimate: N^under=p^⋅N=0.6154⋅50=30.77≈31\hat{N}_{\text{under}} = \hat{p} \cdot N = 0.6154 \cdot 50 = 30.77 \approx 31N^under​=p^​⋅N=0.6154⋅50=30.77≈31 portfolios.

Scenario B: Average Income Confidence Interval​

Problem: A sample of n=250n = 250n=250 twenty-seven-year-olds in London has an average income of Xˉ=45,000\bar{X} = 45,000Xˉ=45,000 GBP with population standard deviation σ=4,000\sigma = 4,000σ=4,000 GBP. Calculate the 95% Confidence Interval.

  1. Given: Xˉ=45000\bar{X} = 45000Xˉ=45000, σ=4000\sigma = 4000σ=4000, n=250n = 250n=250, α=0.05  ⟹  z0.025=1.96\alpha = 0.05 \implies z_{0.025} = 1.96α=0.05⟹z0.025​=1.96
  2. Standard Error: SE=σn=4000250=400015.811≈252.98\text{SE} = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}} = \frac{4000}{\sqrt{250}} = \frac{4000}{15.811} \approx 252.98SE=n​σ​=250​4000​=15.8114000​≈252.98
  3. Margin of Error: E=1.96⋅252.98=495.84E = 1.96 \cdot 252.98 = 495.84E=1.96⋅252.98=495.84
  4. Interval: CI=45000±495.84=[44504.16,  45495.84]\text{CI} = 45000 \pm 495.84 = [44504.16, \; 45495.84]CI=45000±495.84=[44504.16,45495.84]
  5. Interpretation: If we repeat this sampling process many times, roughly 95% of the calculated intervals will contain the true mean income. (It is incorrect to say there is a 95% chance that the true mean is inside this specific fixed interval, as the parameter μ\muμ is a fixed constant, not a random variable).

8. Implementation Lab​

import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

# 1. Confidence Interval for Mean (London Income Scenario)
n = 250
mean_val = 45000
std_val = 4000
conf_level = 0.95

# Calculate scale = std / sqrt(n)
ci_mean = stats.norm.interval(conf_level, loc=mean_val, scale=std_val / np.sqrt(n))
print(f"95% CI for Mean: {ci_mean}")

# 2. Confidence Interval for Proportion (Portfolios Scenario)
n_port = 13
x_port = 8
p_hat = x_port / n_port
se_prop = np.sqrt((p_hat * (1 - p_hat)) / n_port)

ci_prop = stats.norm.interval(0.99, loc=p_hat, scale=se_prop)
print(f"99% CI for Proportion: {ci_prop}")

info

Key Takeaways

  • CLT Guarantees Normality: The sampling distribution of the mean is normal for n≥30n \ge 30n≥30, even if the original population distribution is non-normal.
  • Confidence vs. Probability: The parameter μ\muμ is fixed. A 95% confidence interval means that across repeated sampling experiments, 95% of constructed intervals capture μ\muμ.
  • Controlling Margin of Error: To cut the margin of error in half, you must quadruple the sample size (n∝1/E2n \propto 1/E^2n∝1/E2).
