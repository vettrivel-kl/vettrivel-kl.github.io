Question 1: Concept of Point Estimation (Average Income)​

Question Details​

What would be the average income of a 27-year-old in London? How would you calculate it?

Step-by-Step Solution​

1. Theoretical Formulation​

Let the random variable XXX represent the annual income of a 27-year-old individual residing in London. The true arithmetic mean income across all such individuals in the target population is denoted by the population parameter μ\muμ:

μ=1N∑i=1NXi\mu = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N X_iμ=N1​∑i=1N​Xi​

where NNN is the total population size of 27-year-olds in London.

2. The Operational Reality (Need for Sampling)​

  • The Trivial Approach: Conduct a complete census by querying every single 27-year-old in London and computing μ\muμ.
  • Practical Infeasibility: A full census is cost-prohibitive, operationally difficult, time-consuming, and subject to non-response bias.
  • The Inferential Solution: Draw a representative Simple Random Sample (SRS) of size nnn from the population.

3. Mathematical Point Estimator​

The sample mean (xˉ\bar{x}xˉ) serves as the best point estimator for the population mean μ\muμ:

μ^=xˉ=1n∑i=1nxi\hat{\mu} = \bar{x} = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n x_iμ^​=xˉ=n1​∑i=1n​xi​

  • Unbiasedness Property: The sample mean is an unbiased estimator of μ\muμ because its expected value equals the parameter: E[xˉ]=μ\mathbb{E}[\bar{x}] = \muE[xˉ]=μ
  • Consistency Property: By the Weak Law of Large Numbers, as sample size n→∞n \to \inftyn→∞, the sample mean converges in probability to μ\muμ: lim⁡n→∞P(∣xˉ−μ∣<ϵ)=1∀ϵ>0\lim_{n \to \infty} P(|\bar{x} - \mu| < \epsilon) = 1 \quad \forall \epsilon > 0limn→∞​P(∣xˉ−μ∣<ϵ)=1∀ϵ>0

Academic Inference: Because obtaining the exact population parameter μ\muμ via census is practically unviable, we draw an unbiased random sample of size nnn, calculate the sample mean xˉ\bar{x}xˉ, and report xˉ\bar{x}xˉ as the point estimate for the true average income of a 27-year-old in London.

Question 7: Destructive Testing & The Rationale for Hypothesis Testing​

Question Details​

A manufacturer produces batteries. He claims that the life of his batteries is 25,500 hours. Can we verify his claim?

Step-by-Step Solution​

1. The Trivial Verification Approach​

The naive way to verify this claim is to measure the operating lifespan of every single battery produced by running each unit until its energy is completely depleted.

2. The Operational Dilemma: Destructive Testing​

Lifespan testing is inherently destructive testing. Running a battery to exhaustion consumes the product entirely.

3. The Statistical Alternative: Hypothesis Testing​

To overcome this dilemma, we execute Statistical Hypothesis Testing:

  1. Formulate Statistical Claims:
    • Null Hypothesis (H0H_0H0​): μ=25,500 hours\mu = 25,500 \text{ hours}μ=25,500 hours (Manufacturer claim is true)
    • Alternative Hypothesis (H1H_1H1​): μ<25,500 hours\mu < 25,500 \text{ hours}μ<25,500 hours (Watchdog claim that batteries underperform)
  2. Representative Sampling: Extract a small, random sample of nnn batteries (e.g., n=50n = 50n=50).
  3. Controlled Depletion: Test only the sample units to failure to compute the sample mean xˉ\bar{x}xˉ and sample standard deviation sss.
  4. Inferential Decision: Evaluate the ZZZ-statistic or ttt-statistic. If the empirical evidence deviates significantly from 25,50025,50025,500 hours beyond random sampling chance, we reject H0H_0H0​; otherwise, we fail to reject H0H_0H0​.

Academic Inference: We cannot verify the claim deterministically across the entire population because testing is destructive. Instead, we statistically test the manufacturer's claim using a representative sample, balancing statistical rigor with inventory preservation.

Question 11: One-Sample Left-Tailed Z-Test (PVC Pipe Thickness)​

Question Details​

A sample of 900 PVC pipes is found to have an average thickness of 12.5 mm. Can we assume that the sample is coming from a normal population with mean 13 mm against that it is less than 13 mm? The population standard deviation is 1 mm. Test the hypothesis using the p-value method at 5% level of significance.

1. Hypothesis Formulation​

  • H0:μ≥13 mmH_0: \mu \ge 13 \text{ mm}H0​:μ≥13 mm (Mean thickness is at least 13 mm)

  • Sample size: n=900n = 900n=900
  • Sample mean: xˉ=12.5 mm\bar{x} = 12.5 \text{ mm}xˉ=12.5 mm

3. Standard Error Calculation​

SE=σn=1900=130≈0.03333 mm\text{SE} = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{900}} = \frac{1}{30} \approx 0.03333 \text{ mm}SE=n​σ​=900​1​=301​≈0.03333 mm

4. Test Statistic (ZcalcZ_{\text{calc}}Zcalc​)​

Zcalc=xˉ−μ0SE=12.5−13.01/30=−0.5×30=−15.0Z_{\text{calc}} = \frac{\bar{x} - \mu_0}{\text{SE}} = \frac{12.5 - 13.0}{1 / 30} = -0.5 \times 30 = \mathbf{-15.0}Zcalc​=SExˉ−μ0​​=1/3012.5−13.0​=−0.5×30=−15.0

Question 12: One-Sample Right-Tailed Z-Test (Food Delivery Time)​

Question 13: One-Sample Mean Test with σ\sigmaσ Unknown (Protein Powder)​

Question Details​

The manager of a packaging process at a protein powder manufacturing plant wants to determine if the protein powder packing process is in control. 1. Hypothesis Formulation​

  • H0:μ=350 gramsH_0: \mu = 350 \text{ grams}H0​:μ=350 grams (Packaging process is in statistical control)
  • H1:μ≠350 gramsH_1: \mu \neq 350 \text{ grams}H1​:μ=350 grams (Packaging process is out of control)
  • Test type: Two-Tailed Test, α=0.05\alpha = 0.05α=0.05.

  • Sample standard deviation: s=15 gramss = 15 \text{ grams}s=15 grams (population σ\sigmaσ unknown)
  • Since n=80≥30n = 80 \ge 30n=80≥30, by Slutsky's theorem and the Central Limit Theorem, the sample standard deviation sss can replace σ\sigmaσ with the standard normal approximation: Zcalc≈xˉ−μ0s/nZ_{\text{calc}} \approx \frac{\bar{x} - \mu_0}{s / \sqrt{n}}Zcalc​≈s/n​xˉ−μ0​​

SE=sn=1580=158.94427≈1.67705 grams\text{SE} = \frac{s}{\sqrt{n}} = \frac{15}{\sqrt{80}} = \frac{15}{8.94427} \approx 1.67705 \text{ grams}SE=n​s​=80​15​=8.9442715​≈1.67705 grams Zcalc=354.5−3501.67705=4.51.67705≈+2.683Z_{\text{calc}} = \frac{354.5 - 350}{1.67705} = \frac{4.5}{1.67705} \approx \mathbf{+2.683}Zcalc​=1.67705354.5−350​=1.677054.5​≈+2.683

Question 14: Two-Sample Z-Test for Independent Means (Hemoglobin Study)​

Question Details​

A study was carried out to understand the amount of hemoglobin in blood for males and females. A random sample of 160 males and 180 females have means of 13 g/dL and 15 g/dL. The two samples have standard deviations of 4.1 g/dL for male donors and 3.5 g/dL for female donors. Can it be said that the population means of hemoglobin are the same for men and women? Use α=0.01\alpha = 0.01α=0.01.

Step-by-Step Derivation​

1. Hypothesis Formulation​

Let μ1\mu_1μ1​ denote the population mean hemoglobin for males, and μ2\mu_2μ2​ denote the population mean hemoglobin for females:

  • H0:μ1=μ2  ⟺  μ1−μ2=0H_0: \mu_1 = \mu_2 \iff \mu_1 - \mu_2 = 0H0​:μ1​=μ2​⟺μ1​−μ2​=0 (Male and female mean hemoglobin levels are equal)
  • H1:μ1≠μ2  ⟺  μ1−μ2≠0H_1: \mu_1 \neq \mu_2 \iff \mu_1 - \mu_2 \neq 0H1​:μ1​=μ2​⟺μ1​−μ2​=0 (Male and female mean hemoglobin levels differ)
  • Test type: Two-Tailed Independent Two-Sample Test, α=0.01\alpha = 0.01α=0.01.

  • Female Cohort (Sample 2): n2=180n_2 = 180n2​=180, xˉ2=15 g/dL\bar{x}_2 = 15 \text{ g/dL}xˉ2​=15 g/dL, s2=3.5 g/dLs_2 = 3.5 \text{ g/dL}s2​=3.5 g/dL

SEdiff=s12n1+s22n2\text{SE}_{\text{diff}} = \sqrt{\frac{s_1^2}{n_1} + \frac{s_2^2}{n_2}}SEdiff​=n1​s12​​+n2​s22​​​ SEdiff=(4.1)2160+(3.5)2180=16.81160+12.25180\text{SE}_{\text{diff}} = \sqrt{\frac{(4.1)^2}{160} + \frac{(3.5)^2}{180}} = \sqrt{\frac{16.81}{160} + \frac{12.25}{180}}SEdiff​=160(4.1)2​+180(3.5)2​​=16016.81​+18012.25​​ SEdiff=0.1050625+0.0680556=0.173118≈0.416075 g/dL\text{SE}_{\text{diff}} = \sqrt{0.1050625 + 0.0680556} = \sqrt{0.173118} \approx \mathbf{0.416075 \text{ g/dL}}SEdiff​=0.1050625+0.0680556​=0.173118​≈0.416075 g/dL

  • P-Value Comparison: p-value=2×P(Z>4.807)≈1.53×10−6≪0.01p\text{-value} = 2 \times P(Z > 4.807) \approx 1.53 \times 10^{-6} \ll 0.01p-value=2×P(Z>4.807)≈1.53×10−6≪0.01
