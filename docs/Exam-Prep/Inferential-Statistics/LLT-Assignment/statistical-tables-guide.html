How to Read Statistical Tables: A Beginner's Visual Guide

When opening a statistical table PDF for the first time, seeing thousands of decimal numbers can feel overwhelming.

This guide breaks down the tables into simple, repeatable visual recipes so you can find the exact value you need during an exam in under 15 seconds.

tip

Official Statistical Tables Reference

Open or download the reference tables PDF to follow along with the examples in this guide:
Open / Download Statistical Tables PDF

1. The Core Mental Model (Why Tables Exist)​

Statistical tables are essentially translation dictionaries. They convert back and forth between two languages:

  1. Distances on the X-Axis (ZZZ or ttt values): How many standard errors an observation sits away from the center.
  2. Areas Under the Curve (Probabilities / α\alphaα): The percentage of data or probability contained in a tail.
       Language 1: Z or t score  <----[ Table ]---->  Language 2: Probability / Area (p-value, α)

The Symmetry Secret​

Because normal and ttt curves are perfectly mirror-symmetric around zero, statistical tables only print positive values. You determine whether the value is positive (+++) or negative (−-−) based on whether your problem is on the right tail or the left tail.

2. Table A.1: The Standard Normal ZZZ-Table (Page 2)​

Table A.1 is used whenever sample sizes are large (n≥30n \ge 30n≥30) or when dealing with proportions.

Anatomy of the ZZZ-Table Grid​

The table tells you the area to the left of a given ZZZ-score: A(z)=P(Z≤z)A(z) = P(Z \le z)A(z)=P(Z≤z).

  • Left Vertical Column: Gives the integer and the first decimal (e.g., 1.9).
  • Top Horizontal Row: Gives the second decimal place (e.g., .06).
  • Intersection Cell: Gives the cumulative probability area (0.50000.50000.5000 to 0.99990.99990.9999).

   ...     ...     ...            ...
   1.9    .9713   .9719   ...   [.9750]  <--- Intersection: P(Z ≤ 1.96) = 0.9750 (97.5%)

Direction 2: Finding a P-Value from a Calculated ZZZ-Score​

When you calculate a test statistic (like Zcal=−2.36Z_{\text{cal}} = -2.36Zcal​=−2.36 in Unit 4, Question 1) and need to find the ppp-value:

Step-by-Step Visual Recipe:​

  1. Drop the negative sign (due to symmetry): Z=2.36Z = 2.36Z=2.36.
  2. Split the number: 2.36=2.3+0.062.36 = \mathbf{2.3} + \mathbf{0.06}2.36=2.3+0.06.
  3. Trace the Grid:
    • Find row 2.3 on the left.
    • Find column .06 on the top.
    • Read the intersection cell: 0.9909.
  4. Find the Single Tail Area:
    Subtract from 111 because the table gives the area to the left: Tail Area=1−0.9909=0.0091\text{Tail Area} = 1 - 0.9909 = \mathbf{0.0091}Tail Area=1−0.9909=0.0091
  5. Check Test Type:
    • If One-Tailed: p-value=0.0091p\text{-value} = 0.0091p-value=0.0091.
    • If Two-Tailed: Multiply by 2: p-value=2×0.0091=0.0182p\text{-value} = 2 \times 0.0091 = \mathbf{0.0182}p-value=2×0.0091=0.0182

3. Table A.2: The ttt-Distribution Table (Page 3)​

The ttt-table is used whenever sample size is small (n<30n < 30n<30) and population standard deviation σ\sigmaσ is unknown.

The Two-Row Header Decoder (The Most Common Trap)​

Look closely at the column headers at the top of Page 3:

Degrees of    Two-tailed test:   10%      5%      2%      1%      0.2%    0.1%
 freedom      One-tailed test:    5%     2.5%     1%     0.5%     0.1%    0.05%

Notice that each column has two labels:

  • A two-tailed 5%5\%5% test splits into two 2.5%2.5\%2.5% tails. That is why Two-tailed: 5% sits in the exact same column as One-tailed: 2.5%!

The "No-Brainer" Rule for Picking the Right Column:​

  • Rule 1: If your problem is a Confidence Interval OR a Two-Tailed Test (≠\neq=):
      ⟹  \implies⟹ Look ONLY at the top header row (Two-tailed test:).
  • Rule 2: If your problem is a One-Tailed Test (>>> or <<<):
      ⟹  \implies⟹ Look ONLY at the second header row (One-tailed test:).

Step-by-Step Lookup Examples Mapped from Your Notes​

Example 1: Confidence Interval (Unit 3, Question 3)​

  • Problem: 95%95\%95% Confidence Interval with sample size n=15n = 15n=15.
  • Step 1 (Degrees of Freedom): df=n−1=15−1=14df = n - 1 = 15 - 1 = \mathbf{14}df=n−1=15−1=14.
  • Step 2 (Select Header): Confidence intervals have two tails. Look at row 1: Two-tailed test: 5% (Column 2).
  • Step 3 (Find Value): Go down row df=14df = 14df=14 to Column 2: Value=2.145\text{Value} = \mathbf{2.145}Value=2.145
  • Result: tc=2.145t_c = 2.145tc​=2.145.

Example 2: Left-Tailed Test (Unit 4, Question 2)​

  • Problem: Left-tailed test (H1:μ<20H_1: \mu < 20H1​:μ<20) at α=0.05\alpha = 0.05α=0.05 with n=30n = 30n=30.
  • Step 1 (Degrees of Freedom): df=30−1=29df = 30 - 1 = \mathbf{29}df=30−1=29.
  • Step 2 (Select Header): This is a one-tailed test. Look at row 2: One-tailed test: 5% (Column 1).
  • Step 3 (Find Value): Go down row df=29df = 29df=29 to Column 1: Magnitude=1.699\text{Magnitude} = 1.699Magnitude=1.699
  • Step 4 (Pick the Sign): Since it is a left-tailed test (<<<), the rejection region is on the negative side: tcrit=−1.699t_{\text{crit}} = \mathbf{-1.699}tcrit​=−1.699

Example 3: Right-Tailed Test (Unit 4, Question 6)​

  • Problem: Right-tailed test (H1:μ>4H_1: \mu > 4H1​:μ>4) at α=0.05\alpha = 0.05α=0.05 with n=12n = 12n=12.

  • Step 4 (Pick the Sign): Since it is a right-tailed test (>>>), keep it positive: tcrit=+1.796t_{\text{crit}} = \mathbf{+1.796}tcrit​=+1.796

Example 4: Two-Sample Independent ttt-Test (Unit 4, Question 12)​

  • Problem: Two strategies with n1=5n_1 = 5n1​=5 and n2=5n_2 = 5n2​=5, two-tailed test at α=0.01\alpha = 0.01α=0.01.

  • Step 3 (Find Value): Go down row df=8df = 8df=8 to Column 4: tcrit=±3.355t_{\text{crit}} = \mathbf{\pm 3.355}tcrit​=±3.355

4. Quick Overview: Table A.3 (FFF-Table) & Table A.4 (χ2\chi^2χ2-Table)​

These tables appear in Pages 4 to 10 of your PDF and are used in later units (ANOVA and Contingency Tables):

Table A.3 (FFF-Distribution, Pages 4–9)​

Used when comparing two variances or conducting ANOVA:

  • Numerator dfdfdf (ν1\nu_1ν1​): Read across the top horizontal columns (1,2,3,…1, 2, 3, \dots1,2,3,…).
  • Denominator dfdfdf (ν2\nu_2ν2​): Read down the left vertical rows (1,2,3,…1, 2, 3, \dots1,2,3,…).
  • Pages are separated by α\alphaα: Page 4 is for 5%5\%5% (α=0.05\alpha = 0.05α=0.05), Page 6 is for 1%1\%1% (α=0.01\alpha = 0.01α=0.01).

Table A.4 (χ2\chi^2χ2-Distribution, Page 10)​

Used for tests of independence in contingency tables:

  • Calculate degrees of freedom: df=(rows−1)(columns−1)df = (\text{rows} - 1)(\text{columns} - 1)df=(rows−1)(columns−1).
  • Locate your dfdfdf row, and match with the α\alphaα column (5%5\%5% or 1%1\%1%).
