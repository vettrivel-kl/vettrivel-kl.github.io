# How to Read Statistical Tables: A Beginner's Visual Guide

When opening a statistical table PDF for the first time, seeing thousands of decimal numbers can feel overwhelming. 

This guide breaks down the tables into **simple, repeatable visual recipes** so you can find the exact value you need during an exam in under 15 seconds.

:::tip

**Official Statistical Tables Reference**

Open or download the reference tables PDF to follow along with the examples in this guide:  
**[Open / Download Statistical Tables PDF](/StatistialTables.pdf)**

:::

## 1. The Core Mental Model (Why Tables Exist)

Statistical tables are essentially **translation dictionaries**. They convert back and forth between two languages:

1.  **Distances on the X-Axis ($Z$ or $t$ values):** How many standard errors an observation sits away from the center.
2.  **Areas Under the Curve (Probabilities / $\alpha$):** The percentage of data or probability contained in a tail.

```
       Language 1: Z or t score  <----[ Table ]---->  Language 2: Probability / Area (p-value, α)
```

### The Symmetry Secret
Because normal and $t$ curves are **perfectly mirror-symmetric around zero**, statistical tables **only print positive values**. You determine whether the value is positive ($+$) or negative ($-$) based on whether your problem is on the right tail or the left tail.

## 2. Table A.1: The Standard Normal $Z$-Table (Page 2)

Table A.1 is used whenever sample sizes are large ($n \ge 30$) or when dealing with proportions.

### Anatomy of the $Z$-Table Grid
The table tells you the **area to the left** of a given $Z$-score: $A(z) = P(Z \le z)$.

*   **Left Vertical Column:** Gives the integer and the first decimal (e.g., `1.9`).
*   **Top Horizontal Row:** Gives the second decimal place (e.g., `.06`).
*   **Intersection Cell:** Gives the cumulative probability area ($0.5000$ to $0.9999$).

```
           0.00    0.01    ...    0.06    ...
   ...     ...     ...            ...
   1.9    .9713   .9719   ...   [.9750]  <--- Intersection: P(Z ≤ 1.96) = 0.9750 (97.5%)
   2.0    .9772   .9778   ...    .9803
```

---

### Direction 1: Finding Critical Values ($Z_{\text{crit}}$) for Confidence Intervals & Tests

> **The Exam Secret:** You almost **never** need to search the main grid for common critical $Z$-values!

Look at the **top right corner of Page 2** in your PDF. There is a shortcut box labeled:  
`Values of z of particular importance:`

| What Your Question Asks For | Confidence Level | Alpha ($\alpha$) | What to Look Up | Exact Table A.1 Value |
| :--- | :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: |
| **90% Confidence Interval** | $90\%$ | $0.10$ | Right tail $5\%$ ($0.05$) | **$Z = \pm 1.645$** |
| **95% Confidence Interval** | $95\%$ | $0.05$ | Right tail $2.5\%$ ($0.025$) | **$Z = \pm 1.960$** |
| **99% Confidence Interval** | $99\%$ | $0.01$ | Right tail $0.5\%$ ($0.005$) | **$Z = \pm 2.576$** |
| **1-Tailed Test at 1%** | - | $0.01$ | Right tail $1\%$ ($0.01$) | **$Z = 2.326$** (or $\pm 2.33$) |

#### Worked Example (Unit 3, Question 1 & 2):
*   **Problem:** Calculate a $95\%$ confidence interval.
*   **Action:** Go to Page 2 $\to$ look at the shortcut box $\to$ find $95\%$ (right tail $2.5\%$) $\to$ read **$Z = 1.960$**. Done!

---

### Direction 2: Finding a P-Value from a Calculated $Z$-Score

When you calculate a test statistic (like $Z_{\text{cal}} = -2.36$ in Unit 4, Question 1) and need to find the $p$-value:

#### Step-by-Step Visual Recipe:
1.  **Drop the negative sign** (due to symmetry): $Z = 2.36$.
2.  **Split the number:** $2.36 = \mathbf{2.3} + \mathbf{0.06}$.
3.  **Trace the Grid:**
    *   Find row **`2.3`** on the left.
    *   Find column **`.06`** on the top.
    *   Read the intersection cell: **`0.9909`**.
4.  **Find the Single Tail Area:**  
    Subtract from $1$ because the table gives the area to the left:
    $$\text{Tail Area} = 1 - 0.9909 = \mathbf{0.0091}$$
5.  **Check Test Type:**
    *   *If One-Tailed:* $p\text{-value} = 0.0091$.
    *   *If Two-Tailed:* Multiply by 2:
        $$p\text{-value} = 2 \times 0.0091 = \mathbf{0.0182}$$

## 3. Table A.2: The $t$-Distribution Table (Page 3)

The $t$-table is used whenever sample size is small ($n < 30$) and population standard deviation $\sigma$ is unknown.

### The Two-Row Header Decoder (The Most Common Trap)
Look closely at the column headers at the top of Page 3:

```
Degrees of    Two-tailed test:   10%      5%      2%      1%      0.2%    0.1%
 freedom      One-tailed test:    5%     2.5%     1%     0.5%     0.1%    0.05%
```

Notice that each column has **two labels**:
*   A two-tailed $5\%$ test splits into two $2.5\%$ tails. That is why **Two-tailed: 5%** sits in the exact same column as **One-tailed: 2.5%**!

#### The "No-Brainer" Rule for Picking the Right Column:
*   **Rule 1:** If your problem is a **Confidence Interval** OR a **Two-Tailed Test** ($\neq$):  
    $\implies$ **Look ONLY at the top header row (`Two-tailed test:`)**.
*   **Rule 2:** If your problem is a **One-Tailed Test** ($>$ or $<$):  
    $\implies$ **Look ONLY at the second header row (`One-tailed test:`)**.

---

### Step-by-Step Lookup Examples Mapped from Your Notes

#### Example 1: Confidence Interval (Unit 3, Question 3)
*   **Problem:** $95\%$ Confidence Interval with sample size $n = 15$.
*   **Step 1 (Degrees of Freedom):** $df = n - 1 = 15 - 1 = \mathbf{14}$.
*   **Step 2 (Select Header):** Confidence intervals have two tails. Look at row 1: **`Two-tailed test: 5%`** (Column 2).
*   **Step 3 (Find Value):** Go down row $df = 14$ to Column 2:
    $$\text{Value} = \mathbf{2.145}$$
*   **Result:** $t_c = 2.145$.

---

#### Example 2: Left-Tailed Test (Unit 4, Question 2)
*   **Problem:** Left-tailed test ($H_a: \mu < 20$) at $\alpha = 0.05$ with $n = 30$.
*   **Step 1 (Degrees of Freedom):** $df = 30 - 1 = \mathbf{29}$.
*   **Step 2 (Select Header):** This is a one-tailed test. Look at row 2: **`One-tailed test: 5%`** (Column 1).
*   **Step 3 (Find Value):** Go down row $df = 29$ to Column 1:
    $$\text{Magnitude} = 1.699$$
*   **Step 4 (Pick the Sign):** Since it is a **left-tailed test** ($<$), the rejection region is on the negative side:
    $$t_{\text{crit}} = \mathbf{-1.699}$$

---

#### Example 3: Right-Tailed Test (Unit 4, Question 6)
*   **Problem:** Right-tailed test ($H_a: \mu > 4$) at $\alpha = 0.05$ with $n = 12$.
*   **Step 1 (Degrees of Freedom):** $df = 12 - 1 = \mathbf{11}$.
*   **Step 2 (Select Header):** One-tailed test $\implies$ **`One-tailed test: 5%`** (Column 1).
*   **Step 3 (Find Value):** Go down row $df = 11$ to Column 1:
    $$\text{Magnitude} = 1.796$$
*   **Step 4 (Pick the Sign):** Since it is a **right-tailed test** ($>$), keep it positive:
    $$t_{\text{crit}} = \mathbf{+1.796}$$

---

#### Example 4: Two-Sample Independent $t$-Test (Unit 4, Question 12)
*   **Problem:** Two strategies with $n_1 = 5$ and $n_2 = 5$, two-tailed test at $\alpha = 0.01$.
*   **Step 1 (Degrees of Freedom):**
    $$df = n_1 + n_2 - 2 = 5 + 5 - 2 = \mathbf{8}$$
*   **Step 2 (Select Header):** Two-tailed test $\implies$ **`Two-tailed test: 1%`** (Column 4).
*   **Step 3 (Find Value):** Go down row $df = 8$ to Column 4:
    $$t_{\text{crit}} = \mathbf{\pm 3.355}$$

### Direction 3: How to "Bracket" a P-Value on the $t$-Table (Table A.2)

Unlike Table A.1, the $t$-distribution table **does not print a continuous grid of every probability**. It only has fixed significance columns ($10\%, 5\%, 2\%, 1\%, 0.2\%, 0.1\%$).

On exams without a statistical calculator, examiners expect you to **bracket (sandwich)** the $p$-value between the two closest columns on your degrees of freedom row:

#### Step-by-Step Bracketing Recipe:
1.  **Locate your Degrees of Freedom row** (e.g., $df = 29$).
2.  **Scan across that row** to find where your calculated $|t_{\text{cal}}|$ sits between two printed numbers.
    *   *Example (Unit 4, Question 2):* For $|t_{\text{cal}}| = 2.74$ on row $df = 29$:
        $$\text{Column 1\% has } 2.462, \quad \text{Column 0.5\% has } 2.756$$
        $$2.462 < \mathbf{2.74} < 2.756$$
3.  **Read the Column Headers directly above:**
    $$\mathbf{0.005 < p\text{-value} < 0.01}$$
4.  **State Your Conclusion:** Because the upper boundary ($0.01$) is already smaller than $\alpha = 0.05$, you prove $p\text{-value} < \alpha$ and **Reject $H_0$**!

## 4. Quick Overview: Table A.3 ($F$-Table) & Table A.4 ($\chi^2$-Table)

These tables appear in Pages 4 to 10 of your PDF and are used in later units (ANOVA and Contingency Tables):

### Table A.3 ($F$-Distribution, Pages 4–9)
Used when comparing two variances or conducting ANOVA:
*   **Numerator $df$ ($\nu_1$):** Read across the **top horizontal columns** ($1, 2, 3, \dots$).
*   **Denominator $df$ ($\nu_2$):** Read down the **left vertical rows** ($1, 2, 3, \dots$).
*   *Pages are separated by $\alpha$:* Page 4 is for $5\%$ ($\alpha = 0.05$), Page 6 is for $1\%$ ($\alpha = 0.01$).

### Table A.4 ($\chi^2$-Distribution, Page 10)
Used for tests of independence in contingency tables:
*   Calculate degrees of freedom: $df = (\text{rows} - 1)(\text{columns} - 1)$.
*   Locate your $df$ row, and match with the $\alpha$ column ($5\%$ or $1\%$).

## 5. Master 10-Second Exam Decision Matrix

Whenever you encounter a table question, follow this direct routing chart:

| What the Exam Asks For | Target Table | Row to Locate | Column to Locate | Sign to Use |
| :--- | :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: |
| **Large Sample Mean / Proportion CI** | **Table A.1 (p. 2)** | Shortcut Box | $90\% \to 1.645, \; 95\% \to 1.960, \; 99\% \to 2.576$ | $\pm$ |
| **Small Sample Mean CI** | **Table A.2 (p. 3)** | $df = n - 1$ | `Two-tailed test:` match $1 - \text{Confidence}$ | $\pm$ |
| **Two-Tailed $t$-Test** | **Table A.2 (p. 3)** | $df$ | `Two-tailed test:` match $\alpha$ ($10\%, 5\%, 1\%$) | $\pm$ |
| **Right-Tailed $t$-Test** | **Table A.2 (p. 3)** | $df$ | `One-tailed test:` match $\alpha$ ($5\%, 1\%$) | **$+$** |
| **Left-Tailed $t$-Test** | **Table A.2 (p. 3)** | $df$ | `One-tailed test:` match $\alpha$ ($5\%, 1\%$) | **$-$** |
| **Two-Sample Independent $t$-Test** | **Table A.2 (p. 3)** | $df = n_1 + n_2 - 2$ | Match test direction and $\alpha$ | $\pm$ (or $+$ / $-$) |
| **Paired $t$-Test** | **Table A.2 (p. 3)** | $df = n_{\text{pairs}} - 1$ | Match test direction and $\alpha$ | $+$ or $-$ |
