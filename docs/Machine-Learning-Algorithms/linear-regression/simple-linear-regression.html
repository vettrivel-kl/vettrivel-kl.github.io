Simple Linear Regression

Topic — The first actual algorithm, and the one worth understanding completely, because almost everything later is a variation on it. This page fits a line to a single feature and works the arithmetic all the way through by hand — two coefficients, five error measures, no estimator doing anything you cannot check on paper.

Regression versus classification​

The distinction from Types of Learning, restated because this is where it starts to matter:

RegressionClassification
PredictsA continuous numberA category from a fixed set
Target typeNumericCategorical
Does "close" count?Yes — ₹52 lakh for a ₹50 lakh house is goodNo — "cat" for a dog is simply wrong
Error measured asA distanceA count of mistakes
Output rangeUnboundedOne of kkk labels

The load-bearing difference is the third row. Because closeness counts, regression can be scored by how far off it was — which is what makes squared error the natural loss, and what makes the whole algebraic solution below possible.

The line, and where its two numbers come from​

One feature, one target, a straight line:

y=b0+b1⋅xy = b_0 + b_1 \cdot xy=b0​+b1​⋅x

SymbolNameMeaning
b1b_1b1​slope / coefficientHow much yyy changes per one-unit increase in xxx
b0b_0b0​interceptThe value of yyy when x=0x = 0x=0

Fitting means choosing b0b_0b0​ and b1b_1b1​ to minimize the sum of squared vertical distances between each point and the line. That phrase is usually where explanations stop. It shouldn't — the two numbers come out of a short calculation you can do on paper, and doing it once removes most of the mystery from everything that follows.

The formulas​

Minimizing ∑(yi−b0−b1xi)2\sum (y_i - b_0 - b_1 x_i)^2∑(yi​−b0​−b1​xi​)2 — differentiate with respect to each coefficient, set both derivatives to zero, solve — gives exactly this:

b1=∑(xi−xˉ)(yi−yˉ)∑(xi−xˉ)2=SxySxxb_1 = \frac{\sum (x_i - \bar{x})(y_i - \bar{y})}{\sum (x_i - \bar{x})^2} = \frac{S_{xy}}{S_{xx}}b1​=∑(xi​−xˉ)2∑(xi​−xˉ)(yi​−yˉ​)​=Sxx​Sxy​​

b0=yˉ−b1⋅xˉb_0 = \bar{y} - b_1 \cdot \bar{x}b0​=yˉ​−b1​⋅xˉ

Two things are worth noticing before computing anything.

b1b_1b1​ is a ratio of sums about the means. The numerator asks how xxx and yyy deviate together; the denominator asks how much xxx deviates at all. If xxx barely varies, the denominator is tiny and the slope is unstable — which is the same defect that makes a constant column fail a pre-flight audit.

b0b_0b0​ is not fitted independently. Once the slope is known, the intercept is forced: the line must pass through (xˉ,yˉ)(\bar{x}, \bar{y})(xˉ,yˉ​). Every least-squares line goes through the mean of the data. That is not a convention, it falls out of the algebra.

How wrong is it? The five error measures​

The fit produces predictions, the predictions produce residuals, and every standard error measure is a different summary of those same residuals. Nothing new is needed — they are all computed from the numbers already calculated in the workbench table.

Two aggregates carry everything else:

  • SSE (Sum of Squared Errors): ∑(yi−y^i)2=2.4\sum (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2 = 2.4∑(yi​−y^​i​)2=2.4 — the quantity the fit minimized.
  • SST (Total Sum of Squares): ∑(yi−yˉ)2=6.0\sum (y_i - \bar{y})^2 = 6.0∑(yi​−yˉ​)2=6.0 — the error you would make predicting the mean every time, which is the baseline any model has to beat.

Three load-bearing insights from these numbers:

  • R2=0.6000R^2 = 0.6000R2=0.6000 represents a comparison, not an accuracy. It means our model has cleared away 60% of the baseline variance (SST) that a constant mean-guesser (yˉ\bar{y}yˉ​) would suffer. It is unbounded below: a negative R2R^2R2 is common on test sets and means your model is worse than predicting the mean.
  • The gap between MAE (0.64000.64000.6400) and RMSE (0.69280.69280.6928) measures outlier severity. Because RMSE squares errors, large outliers pull it upward, widening the gap. A wide gap signals that a few massive misses are dominating your model's performance.
  • Adjusted R2R^2R2 falls severely (0.6000→0.46670.6000 \to 0.46670.6000→0.4667) due to small sample size. With n=5n = 5n=5 and k=1k = 1k=1, the degree-of-freedom penalty n−1n−k−1=1.33\frac{n-1}{n-k-1} = 1.33n−k−1n−1​=1.33 is extremely punitive. On realistic datasets with large nnn, this gap shrinks unless you flood the model with useless noise columns.
note

Choosing between them is a separate question

This page computes the five so the arithmetic is never mysterious. Which one to report — and the traps in each, including a genuine sign inversion in scikit-learn's cross-validation scoring — belongs with regression performance metrics, along with residual diagnostics. Compute first, choose later.

r, b₁ and R² are three views of one fact​

For a one-feature regression with an intercept, the correlation coefficient and the fit are not separate pieces of information. Two exact identities:

b1=r×sysxR2=r2b_1 = r \times \frac{s_y}{s_x} \qquad \qquad R^2 = r^2b1​=r×sx​sy​​R2=r2

On the five default points above, r=0.774597r = 0.774597r=0.774597, and both hold to every digit printed:

  • r × (s_y/s_x) = 0.600000 (equal to b1b_1b1​)
  • r² = 0.600000 (equal to R2R^2R2)

The ddofddofddof question — population or sample standard deviation — is the one people expect to matter here, and it does not: the identity is exact either way because the denominator cancels out of the algebra. One less thing to get wrong.

The first identity says a correlation becomes a slope once you rescale it by the spread of the two variables. The second is narrower than it looks:

danger

R2=r2R^2 = r^2R2=r2 holds only for simple regression

With one feature and an intercept, R2R^2R2 is the squared correlation between xxx and yyy. With two or more features it is not — there is no single rrr to square, and R2R^2R2 becomes the squared correlation between yyy and the model's predictions y^\hat{y}y^​. Carrying the R2=r2R^2 = r^2R2=r2 intuition into multiple regression is a common and quiet error.

How much data does it take? The 1/√n Law​

The recovered coefficients are never exactly right, because noise in real-world data is real. The useful question is how fast the estimation error shrinks as we collect more rows. Theory is specific: the standard deviation of the estimated slope is:

SD(b^1)=σn⋅Var(x)SD(\hat{b}_1) = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n \cdot Var(x)}}SD(b^1​)=n⋅Var(x)​σ​

Thus, the error falls as 1/n1/\sqrt{n}1/n​, and RMSE×nRMSE \times \sqrt{n}RMSE×n​ should be a constant. With a true noise σ=2\sigma = 2σ=2 and x∼Uniform(0,10)x \sim Uniform(0, 10)x∼Uniform(0,10), the variance of xxx is 100/12=8.333100 / 12 = 8.333100/12=8.333, making that constant exactly 2/8.333=0.69282 / \sqrt{8.333} = 0.69282/8.333​=0.6928.

Averaging over many fits per nnn is not statistical decoration here; it is the difference between the right conclusion and a false one.

What "linear" actually restricts​

The most common misreading of linear regression is that it can only fit straight lines. It cannot fit curves in its coefficients — a completely different restriction.

The data below is a parabola, y=2x2−3x+5y = 2x^2 - 3x + 5y=2x2−3x+5 plus noise:

Output
5. 'LINEAR' MEANS LINEAR IN THE COEFFICIENTS
   given x            R² = 0.4559   coefficients [-2.8500]
   given x, x²        R² = 0.9826   coefficients [-3.0394  1.9711]
   given x, x², x³    R² = 0.9826   coefficients [-3.1011  1.9705  0.0113]

Given only xxx, the model manages R2=0.4559R^2 = 0.4559R2=0.4559 — it is trying to fit a curve with a line. Given xxx and x2x^2x2 as two columns, it reaches 0.9826, and its coefficients [-3.0394, 1.9711] recover the true −3-3−3 and 222.

Nothing about the estimator changed. x2x^2x2 is just another column of numbers, and the model is still computing a weighted sum:

y=b0+b1⋅x+b2⋅(x2)y = b_0 + b_1 \cdot x + b_2 \cdot (x^2)y=b0​+b1​⋅x+b2​⋅(x2)

which is linear in b0,b1,b2b_0, b_1, b_2b0​,b1​,b2​ even though it draws a parabola. Adding x3x^3x3 gained nothing — R2R^2R2 is identical at 0.98260.98260.9826, and the new coefficient came out near zero (0.01130.01130.0113).

tip

"Linear" is a statement about the coefficients, not the shape

A model is linear if the prediction is a weighted sum of its inputs — whatever those inputs are.

This is why feature engineering is so powerful here: x2,log⁡(x),x1×x2x^2, \log(x), x_1 \times x_2x2,log(x),x1​×x2​, and sin⁡(2πh/24)\sin(2\pi h / 24)sin(2πh/24) are all just columns, and each extends what a linear model can express without changing the model at all. It is also why adding interaction terms can raise R2R^2R2 dramatically.

What linear regression genuinely cannot do is discover those columns for itself.

The assumptions​

Linear regression is a model of the world, and it assumes four things.

AssumptionMeansViolated when
LinearityThe relationship really is a weighted sumCurvature is left in the residuals
IndependenceRows do not influence each otherTime series; repeated measures per subject
HomoscedasticityError spread is constant across the rangeErrors fan out as yyy grows
Normality of residualsErrors are roughly normally distributedNeeded for confidence intervals, not for fitting

Two are worth stressing. Independence is the same requirement that made a random train/test split invalid on grouped data — one violation, two consequences. And normality of residuals is often over-emphasized: the coefficients are unbiased without it. It matters for inference, not for prediction.

Checking them is residual work — plotting residuals against fitted values and looking for pattern, spread, or curvature — which belongs with regression performance metrics.

Correlation with the target, and why it misleads​

The obvious way to decide whether a feature is worth having is to correlate it with the target. Fit each of three features on its own against house price, where the truth is:

price=3000⋅size−800⋅age+50000\text{price} = 3000 \cdot \text{size} - 800 \cdot \text{age} + 50000price=3000⋅size−800⋅age+50000

and door_colour is pure noise:

Output

   size            +0.9849     3043.86    0.9700

size behaves as expected: correlation +0.9849+0.9849+0.9849 and R2=0.9700R^2 = 0.9700R2=0.9700. The other two rows are the interesting ones.

  • age has a real effect of −800-800−800 per year and a marginal correlation of −0.0392-0.0392−0.0392 (essentially zero). Its solo R2R^2R2 is 0.0015. On this evidence you would drop it!
  • door_colour is meaningless and gets a fitted coefficient of 5160.695160.695160.69 and solo R2R^2R2 of 0.0018 — which is actually higher than age's!

Why the useful feature is invisible​

Not a mystery. Price variance is dominated by other terms:

  • size spread: 150 units×3000=450,000150 \text{ units} \times 3000 = 450,000150 units×3000=450,000
  • age spread: 50 units×800=40,00050 \text{ units} \times 800 = 40,00050 units×800=40,000
  • noise: σ=20,000\sigma = 20,000σ=20,000

age's effect is real but roughly a tenth of size's. Correlating age against raw price asks it to show up against all of that giant size variation at once.

tip

This is what "holding all else constant" buys you

A coefficient in a multiple regression is measured on exactly this basis — the variation left over once the other features have been accounted for. That is why a multiple regression can find an effect that no marginal correlation shows, and it is the single strongest argument for fitting features jointly rather than screening them one at a time.

3. Common Mistakes​

  • 1. Linear = Straight Lines Only
  • 2. Independent Intercept
  • 3. Marginal Feature Screening
  • 4. Adjusted R² function

  • The Misconception: "I thought linear regression could only fit straight lines in 2D space."
  • Wrong Thinking: Assuming you cannot fit curved data trends like parabolas, exponentials, or sinusoids.
  • The Right Principle: "Linear" refers to being linear in the coefficients (b0,b1,b2b_0, b_1, b_2b0​,b1​,b2​), not the feature shape. By feeding x2x^2x2 or log⁡(x)\log(x)log(x) as new columns, the model fits complex curves perfectly while remaining a simple linear estimator.

  • The Misconception: "I thought the intercept b0b_0b0​ is optimized completely independently of the slope b1b_1b1​."
  • Wrong Thinking: Believing the optimizer picks both parameters freely.
  • The Right Principle: Once the slope b1b_1b1​ is computed, the intercept is locked as b0=yˉ−b1xˉb_0 = \bar{y} - b_1\bar{x}b0​=yˉ​−b1​xˉ. Every least-squares regression line must pass through the mean coordinates (xˉ,yˉ)(\bar{x}, \bar{y})(xˉ,yˉ​).

  • The Misconception: "If a feature has almost zero correlation with the target, it is useless and should be dropped."
  • Wrong Thinking: Screening out features (like age in our housing dataset) based on their solo correlation coefficient with yyy.
  • The Right Principle: High-variance features can completely drown out weaker but important predictors. Always fit features jointly; multiple regression automatically measures each coefficient after holding the others constant.

  • The Misconception: "I'll just import adjusted_r2_score from sklearn.metrics."
  • Wrong Thinking: Spending hours looking for a non-existent scikit-learn function.
  • The Right Principle: Scikit-learn has no Adjusted R2R^2R2 function. You must compute it yourself using 1−(1−R2)(n−1)n−k−11 - \frac{(1-R^2)(n-1)}{n-k-1}1−n−k−1(1−R2)(n−1)​.

4. Summary​

Core Theory

  • Least Squares Fit: Minimizes the sum of squared vertical residuals (SSE).
  • The Force Rule: The line is mathematically forced to pass through the mean coordinate point (xˉ,yˉ)(\bar{x}, \bar{y})(xˉ,yˉ​).
  • R2R^2R2 Meaning: Compares the model's performance against predicting the target mean (yˉ\bar{y}yˉ​) every single time.
  • R2=r2R^2 = r^2R2=r2: Holds true for simple (one-feature) regression only.

Engineering Realities

  • Leverage Trap: Points at x=xˉx = \bar{x}x=xˉ have zero influence on slope. Outliers far from the mean have severe leverage.
  • The 1/n1/\sqrt{n}1/n​ Law: Shrinking the estimation error by half requires four times the training data.
  • Masked Features: A vital feature's correlation can be masked entirely by a high-variance feature unless evaluated jointly.
  • No Sklearn Adjusted R2R^2R2: Must be written manually to avoid importing non-existent metric functions.

info

Key Takeaways

  • Convexity: Squared error is chosen because it is convex and differentiable, leading to a closed-form analytical solution.
  • Outliers: Squaring the residuals makes the line highly sensitive to outlier values, especially those at high-leverage positions.
  • Linear Limitations: Linear models cannot discover feature interactions or transformations (like x2x^2x2); they must be engineered manually.

Review Flashcards​

1. [THEORY] Why is the intercept b0b_0b0​ not fitted independently of b1b_1b1​?

  • The Derivation: The least-squares loss is L=∑(yi−b0−b1xi)2L = \sum (y_i - b_0 - b_1 x_i)^2L=∑(yi​−b0​−b1​xi​)2.
  • Taking the partial derivative with respect to b0b_0b0​ gives: ∂L∂b0=−2∑(yi−b0−b1xi)=0\frac{\partial L}{\partial b_0} = -2 \sum (y_i - b_0 - b_1 x_i) = 0∂b0​∂L​=−2∑(yi​−b0​−b1​xi​)=0
  • Dividing by −2-2−2 and expanding the sum: ∑yi−n⋅b0−b1∑xi=0\sum y_i - n \cdot b_0 - b_1 \sum x_i = 0∑yi​−n⋅b0​−b1​∑xi​=0
  • Dividing through by nnn: yˉ−b0−b1xˉ=0  ⟹  b0=yˉ−b1xˉ\bar{y} - b_0 - b_1 \bar{x} = 0 \implies b_0 = \bar{y} - b_1 \bar{x}yˉ​−b0​−b1​xˉ=0⟹b0​=yˉ​−b1​xˉ
  • The Reality: This mathematical proof guarantees that the fitted regression line is always forced to pass through the exact mean point (xˉ,yˉ)(\bar{x}, \bar{y})(xˉ,yˉ​).
2. [ANALYZE] How do outliers far from the mean of xxx affect the slope compared to outliers near the mean of xxx?

  • Leverage Principle: The denominator of b1b_1b1​ is Sxx=∑(xi−xˉ)2S_{xx} = \sum (x_i - \bar{x})^2Sxx​=∑(xi​−xˉ)2.
  • The influence (or leverage) of any point on the slope is proportional to its squared distance from the mean of xxx, (xi−xˉ)2(x_i - \bar{x})^2(xi​−xˉ)2.
  • If a point sits exactly at xi=xˉx_i = \bar{x}xi​=xˉ, its (xi−xˉ)=0(x_i - \bar{x}) = 0(xi​−xˉ)=0, meaning it has zero leverage over the slope b1b_1b1​ (it only affects the vertical position of the line, i.e., b0b_0b0​).
  • An outlier far out on the xxx-axis has enormous (xi−xˉ)2(x_i - \bar{x})^2(xi​−xˉ)2, acting as a heavy anchor that can single-handedly pivot the entire slope of the line.
3. [THEORY] What baseline does R2R^2R2 compare the model against, and how can R2R^2R2 be negative?

  • The Baseline: R2R^2R2 compares the model's SSE against the Sum of Squares Total (SST), where SST=∑(yi−yˉ)2SST = \sum (y_i - \bar{y})^2SST=∑(yi​−yˉ​)2. SST represents the error of a "constant baseline model" that always predicts the mean yˉ\bar{y}yˉ​.
  • Negative R2R^2R2: Since R2=1−SSESSTR^2 = 1 - \frac{SSE}{SST}R2=1−SSTSSE​, it becomes negative when SSE>SSTSSE > SSTSSE>SST.
  • This occurs when the model's predictions make larger errors than the simple constant mean predictor. This is common when testing a model on unseen out-of-sample data, proving the model is worse than useless.
4. [ANALYZE] If a feature is highly predictive of the target, why might its correlation with the target look near-zero?

  • The Masking Effect: Correlation measures marginal (one-on-one) relationship. If there is a dominant feature that accounts for 90% of the target's variance, a secondary but highly useful feature's signal is completely buried under that massive leftover variance.
  • The Solution: Subtracting the dominant feature's contribution ("partialing out" the effect) reveals the true, strong correlation of the secondary feature. Multiple regression does this automatically by fitting all coefficients jointly.
5. [THEORY] What does "linear" in linear regression actually restrict? Can it fit a curve?

  • The Restriction: "Linear" restricts the model to be linear in its coefficients (b0,b1,b2b_0, b_1, b_2b0​,b1​,b2​), not linear in its input shapes.
  • Fitting Curves: It can fit a curve (like a parabola y=b0+b1x+b2x2y = b_0 + b_1 x + b_2 x^2y=b0​+b1​x+b2​x2) because x2x^2x2 is treated by the estimator as just another column of numeric values. The formula remains a simple weighted sum of inputs.
  • The Limitation: A linear model cannot discover these transformations itself; they must be engineered as features manually.
