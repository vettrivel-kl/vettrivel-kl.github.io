# F-Scores and Balanced Metrics

> **Topic —** Because Precision and Recall exist in a continuous mechanical tradeoff, comparing competing models requires a unified scalar benchmark. This guide explores the **$F_1$-Score**, derives why the **harmonic mean** is mathematically mandatory over the arithmetic mean, explores domain weighting with **$F_\beta$** ($F_{0.5}$ and $F_2$), and demonstrates why **Balanced Accuracy** and the **Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC)** are superior on imbalanced data.

---

## 1. The Core Concept

When comparing models across validation folds, evaluating two separate numbers (e.g. Model A: $P=90\%, R=50\%$ vs. Model B: $P=70\%, R=75\%$) creates ambiguity. 

The **$F_1$-Score** resolves this by computing the **harmonic mean** of Precision and Recall:

$$F_1 = 2 \cdot \frac{\text{Precision} \cdot \text{Recall}}{\text{Precision} + \text{Recall}}$$

### Why the Harmonic Mean?

The arithmetic mean $\frac{P + R}{2}$ treats errors additively and permits **compensatory masking**: a model with $100\%$ precision that catches only $1$ out of $1,000$ actual positives ($R = 0.1\%$) receives an arithmetic mean of $\approx 50\%$.

The harmonic mean is defined as the **reciprocal of the arithmetic mean of reciprocals**:

$$\frac{1}{F_1} = \frac{1}{2} \left( \frac{1}{\text{Precision}} + \frac{1}{\text{Recall}} \right)$$

Because reciprocals explode as values approach zero ($\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{1}{x} = \infty$), **the harmonic mean is dominated by the minimum value**. If either Precision or Recall collapses toward zero, $F_1$ crashes to zero.

:::tip

**If you only take one thing from this page**

The arithmetic mean rewards models that sacrifice one metric to inflate the other. The **Harmonic Mean ($F_1$) penalizes imbalance**, strictly demanding that a model perform well across *both* Precision and Recall to achieve a high score.

:::

## 3. Weighting Asymmetric Priorities with F-Beta

When operational costs are asymmetric, the parameter $\beta$ in the generalized **$F_\beta$-Score** allows engineers to tilt evaluation weight toward Precision or Recall:

$$F_\beta = (1 + \beta^2) \cdot \frac{\text{Precision} \cdot \text{Recall}}{\beta^2 \cdot \text{Precision} + \text{Recall}}$$

<Tabs>
  <TabItem value="f1" label="1. F1-Score (β = 1.0)" default>
    <br/>
    
    *   **Weighting:** Precision and Recall are weighted equally.
    *   **Formula:** $F_1 = 2 \cdot \frac{P \cdot R}{P + R}$.
    *   **Optimal For:** General benchmark ranking when false positive and false negative costs are comparable.
  </TabItem>
  <TabItem value="f2" label="2. F2-Score (β = 2.0)">
    <br/>
    
    *   **Weighting:** **Weights Recall twice as heavily as Precision** ($\beta^2 = 4$).
    *   **Formula:** $F_2 = 5 \cdot \frac{P \cdot R}{4P + R}$.
    *   **Optimal For:**
        *   Medical cancer detection (missing disease is unacceptable).
        *   Wire fraud monitoring (catching theft outweighs transaction reviews).
  </TabItem>
  <TabItem value="f05" label="3. F0.5-Score (β = 0.5)">
    <br/>
    
    *   **Weighting:** **Weights Precision twice as heavily as Recall** ($\beta^2 = 0.25$).
    *   **Formula:** $F_{0.5} = 1.25 \cdot \frac{P \cdot R}{0.25P + R}$.
    *   **Optimal For:**
        *   Automated spam email deletion (destroying legitimate email is intolerable).
        *   High-confidence loan approvals.
  </TabItem>
</Tabs>

## 4. Balanced Metrics for Severe Imbalance: Balanced Accuracy & MCC

While $F_1$ is far superior to overall accuracy, it has a blind spot: **$F_1$ ignores True Negatives completely**.

If a model accurately classifies 10,000 legitimate negative instances, the $F_1$ formula never inspects the $\text{TN}$ quadrant. For extreme class imbalance, two alternative metrics provide complete matrix coverage:

### 1. Balanced Accuracy

Computes the unweighted arithmetic mean of Recall (TPR) and Specificity (TNR):

$$\text{Balanced Accuracy} = \frac{\text{Recall} + \text{Specificity}}{2} = \frac{1}{2} \left( \frac{\text{TP}}{\text{TP} + \text{FN}} + \frac{\text{TN}}{\text{TN} + \text{FP}} \right)$$

*   On balanced data, Balanced Accuracy equals standard Accuracy.
*   A trivial majority classifier always scores exactly **$50.0\%$**, instantly revealing lack of skill.

### 2. Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC)

The **Matthews Correlation Coefficient** treats binary classification as a Pearson product-moment correlation between actual and predicted binary vectors:

$$\text{MCC} = \frac{\text{TP} \cdot \text{TN} - \text{FP} \cdot \text{FN}}{\sqrt{(\text{TP} + \text{FP})(\text{TP} + \text{FN})(\text{TN} + \text{FP})(\text{TN} + \text{FN})}}$$

*   **Range:** $[-1.0, +1.0]$.
    *   $+1.0$: Perfect classification.
    *   $0.0$: Equivalent to random guessing or predicting a constant majority class.
    *   $-1.0$: Total inverse disagreement.
*   **Completeness:** MCC is the **only metric that uses all four quadrants** symmetrically in both the numerator and denominator.

## 6. Common Mistakes

<Tabs>
  <TabItem value="m1" label="🚨 1. Using the Arithmetic Mean for F1" default>
    <br/>
    
    *   **The Misconception:** *"The F1-score is simply the average of precision and recall: (P + R) / 2."*
    *   **❌ Wrong Thinking:** Computing the arithmetic mean instead of the harmonic mean.
    *   **✅ The Right Principle:** $F_1 = 2 \frac{P \cdot R}{P + R}$. The arithmetic mean masks catastrophic failure when one metric collapses to near zero.
  </TabItem>
  <TabItem value="m2" label="🚨 2. Inverting Beta Weights in F-Beta">
    <br/>
    
    *   **The Misconception:** *"F2 prioritizes precision because 2 is greater than 1."*
    *   **❌ Wrong Thinking:** Assuming $\beta > 1$ weights precision.
    *   **✅ The Right Principle:** $\beta$ represents how many times more important **Recall** is compared to Precision. $F_2$ ($\beta=2$) weights Recall twice as much as Precision. $F_{0.5}$ ($\beta=0.5$) weights Precision twice as much as Recall.
  </TabItem>
  <TabItem value="m3" label="🚨 3. Assuming F1 Cares About True Negatives">
    <br/>
    
    *   **The Misconception:** *"A high F1-score proves the model accurately handles both positive and negative classes."*
    *   **❌ Wrong Thinking:** Forgetting that TN is absent from the F1 formula.
    *   **✅ The Right Principle:** $F_1$ only inspects $TP, FP, FN$. It provides zero verification of negative class accuracy. To evaluate performance across both classes symmetrically on imbalanced datasets, report **Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC)** or Balanced Accuracy.
  </TabItem>
  <TabItem value="m4" label="🚨 4. Comparing F1 Across Differing Class Prevalences">
    <br/>
    
    *   **The Misconception:** *"An F1 score of 0.70 means the same thing on a 50:50 dataset as on a 99:1 dataset."*
    *   **❌ Wrong Thinking:** Treating $F_1$ as an absolute scale-invariant metric.
    *   **✅ The Right Principle:** On a 99:1 dataset, baseline precision is $1\%$, making an $F_1$ of $0.70$ an extraordinary achievement. On a balanced dataset, random guessing yields $F_1 \approx 0.50$. Always anchor $F_1$ against class distribution.
  </TabItem>
  <TabItem value="m5" label="🚨 5. Zero Denominator Crashes in MCC">
    <br/>
    
    *   **The Misconception:** *"MCC can always be calculated for any classifier output."*
    *   **❌ Wrong Thinking:** Failing to handle degenerate models that predict only one class.
    *   **✅ The Right Principle:** If a model predicts 'Negative' for every sample ($TP = FP = 0$), the MCC denominator $\sqrt{(TP+FP)\dots}$ evaluates to zero. Scikit-learn handles this edge case by returning $0.0$.
  </TabItem>
</Tabs>

### Review Flashcards

<details>
  <summary>❓ <b>1. [THEORY] Why is the harmonic mean of two non-zero numbers always less than or equal to their arithmetic mean?</b></summary>
  <div className="answer-content">
    <br/>
    
    *   **The AM-GM-HM Inequality:** For any positive real numbers $a$ and $b$:
        $$\text{Arithmetic Mean } \ge \text{ Geometric Mean } \ge \text{ Harmonic Mean}$$
        $$\frac{a + b}{2} \ge \sqrt{ab} \ge \frac{2ab}{a + b}$$
    *   Equality holds if and only if $a = b$ (Precision equals Recall).
    *   The greater the divergence between Precision and Recall, the larger the penalty gap between Arithmetic and Harmonic mean.
  </div>
</details>

<details>
  <summary>❓ <b>2. [MATH] A model achieves Precision = 0.80 and Recall = 0.60. Calculate F1 by hand.</b></summary>
  <div className="answer-content">
    <br/>
    
    *   **Product:** $P \cdot R = 0.80 \times 0.60 = 0.48$.
    *   **Sum:** $P + R = 0.80 + 0.60 = 1.40$.
    *   **F1 Calculation:**
        $$F_1 = 2 \cdot \frac{0.48}{1.40} = \frac{0.96}{1.40} = \frac{96}{140} = \frac{24}{35} \approx 0.6857 \quad (68.6\%)$$
    *   Notice $F_1 \approx 68.6\%$ is lower than the Arithmetic Mean ($70.0\%$).
  </div>
</details>

<details>
  <summary>❓ <b>3. [ANALYZE] Why can Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC) identify a useless model on imbalanced data when F1 cannot?</b></summary>
  <div className="answer-content">
    <br/>
    
    *   **Missing True Negatives:** $F_1$ only inspects $TP, FP, FN$.
    *   If a dataset has 9,900 negatives and 100 positives, a naive model that predicts positive for 500 samples (including 80 actual positives and 420 false alarms) gets:
        *   $P = 80 / 500 = 16\%$, $R = 80 / 100 = 80\% \implies F_1 \approx 26.7\%$.
    *   MCC evaluates all four cells ($TP=80, FP=420, FN=20, TN=9480$):
        *   Because the model generated 420 false alarms on the negative class, MCC reflects this error in its denominator and yields a near-zero correlation ($\text{MCC} \approx 0.35$).
  </div>
</details>

<details>
  <summary>❓ <b>4. [PRACTICE] How does Balanced Accuracy prevent the Accuracy Paradox on an imbalanced dataset (99% negative, 1% positive)?</b></summary>
  <div className="answer-content">
    <br/>
    
    *   A trivial model that always predicts Negative gets:
        *   $\text{Recall} = 0 / 1 = 0.0$ ($0\%$).
        *   $\text{Specificity} = 99 / 99 = 1.0$ ($100\%$).
    *   **Balanced Accuracy:**
        $$\text{Balanced Accuracy} = \frac{0.0 + 1.0}{2} = 0.50 \quad (50.0\%)$$
    *   While standard accuracy reports an inflated $99.0\%$, Balanced Accuracy reveals that the model performs no better than a random coin toss ($50\%$).
  </div>
</details>

<details>
  <summary>❓ <b>5. [THEORY] Can F1 ever exceed both Precision and Recall?</b></summary>
  <div className="answer-content">
    <br/>
    
    *   **No, never:** Because $F_1$ is a mean (specifically a harmonic mean), it is mathematically constrained to lie strictly between the two values:
        $$\min(\text{Precision}, \text{Recall}) \le F_1 \le \max(\text{Precision}, \text{Recall})$$
    *   $F_1$ equals Precision and Recall if and only if Precision equals Recall ($P = R \implies F_1 = P$).
  </div>
</details>
