R², Adjusted R² and MAPE

Topic — The previous page gave you three error averages that live in the units of y: MAE, MSE and RMSE. All three need context before they mean anything. This page covers the metrics that carry their own context: R², which compares your model against the laziest possible model, Adjusted R², which charges rent for every feature you add, and MAPE, which reports error as a percentage. It settles four things: why R² is not always "the fraction of variance explained", why SST = SSR + SSE is a conditional identity rather than a law, why R² alone can never tell you a model has too many features, and why MAPE quietly rewards guessing low.

In plain words​

Suppose you must predict house prices and you are not allowed to look at a single feature. The best you can do is guess the average price for every house. That guess is the baseline — the laziest defensible model. R² measures how much of the baseline's error your model got rid of.

  • R² = 0 → you did exactly as well as guessing the average. Your features bought nothing.
  • R² = 1 → no error left. Every prediction is exact.
  • R² = 0.85 → you removed 85% of the baseline's squared error.
  • R² < 0 → you did worse than guessing the average. This is allowed, and it happens.

That last point surprises people. The name "coefficient of determination" and the square in the symbol both suggest a number trapped in [0, 1]. It is not bounded below at all.

Adjusted R² exists because plain R² has one specific defect. Add a column of random noise to your features, refit, and R² goes up — never down, not by a hair. So R² can never tell you a model is carrying features it does not need. Adjusted R² charges a fee per feature, paid in degrees of freedom; a feature that does not earn more than its fee makes the score fall.

MAPE answers a different question: how wrong am I as a percentage of the true value? An error of ₹2 lakh on a ₹20 lakh house is 10%; the same ₹2 lakh on a ₹2 crore house is 1%. MAPE treats those rows differently where MAE treats them identically.

tip

If you only take one thing from this page

R² compares your model against one specific rival: predicting the mean for every row. Everything strange about R² — negative values, rising when you add junk, changing when you re-split — follows from the fact that the rival changes too. Different rows, different mean, different SST, therefore a different R² for the same predictions.

R² asks a ratio question, not a magnitude question​

MAE, MSE and RMSE answer "how big is the error?" and carry the units of y. R² answers "how big is the error compared to doing nothing?" and carries no units at all.

R2=1−SSESST=1−∑(yi−y^i)2∑(yi−yˉ)2R^2 = 1 - \frac{\text{SSE}}{\text{SST}} = 1 - \frac{\sum (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2}{\sum (y_i - \bar{y})^2}R2=1−SSTSSE​=1−∑(yi​−yˉ​)2∑(yi​−y^​i​)2​

Read the fraction alone: SSE/SST is the share of the baseline's squared error that survived your model. Subtract from one and you have the share you removed.

SSE/SSTR²Meaning
0.001.00No error left
0.020.98You removed 98% of the baseline's squared error
1.000.00You matched the baseline exactly
1.66−0.66You produced 66% more squared error than the baseline

Because units cancel, R² reads the same whether y is in rupees, kelvin or millimetres. That is the appeal, and also the danger: a unitless number is easy to quote without checking what baseline it was measured against.

note

R² is dimensionless but not portable across datasets — it depends on SST, which depends on how spread out y is in the rows you scored. Quote R² and RMSE together, or you have told half the story: why the same RMSE is excellent or useless.

The identity that fails here​

Textbooks state SST=SSR+SSESST = SSR + SSESST=SSR+SSE, then define R2R^2R2 a second way as SSR/SSTSSR/SSTSSR/SST. Try both:

SSR+SSE=1580.80+30.00=1610.80≠1322.80=SST\text{SSR} + \text{SSE} = 1580.80 + 30.00 = 1610.80 \ne 1322.80 = \text{SST}SSR+SSE=1580.80+30.00=1610.80=1322.80=SST

SSR/SST=1580.80/1322.80=1.1951(an R2 above 1!)\text{SSR} / \text{SST} = 1580.80 / 1322.80 = 1.1951 \qquad \text{(an } R^2 \text{ above 1!)}SSR/SST=1580.80/1322.80=1.1951(an R2 above 1!)

The reason is algebra, not arithmetic. Write y−yˉ=(y^−yˉ)+(y−y^)y - \bar{y} = (\hat{y} - \bar{y}) + (y - \hat{y})y−yˉ​=(y^​−yˉ​)+(y−y^​) and square the sum:

∑(y−yˉ)2=∑(y^−yˉ)2+∑(y−y^)2+2∑(y^−yˉ)(y−y^)\sum(y - \bar{y})^2 = \sum(\hat{y} - \bar{y})^2 + \sum(y - \hat{y})^2 + 2\sum(\hat{y} - \bar{y})(y - \hat{y})∑(y−yˉ​)2=∑(y^​−yˉ​)2+∑(y−y^​)2+2∑(y^​−yˉ​)(y−y^​)

SST=SSR+SSE+2⋅(cross term)\text{SST} = \text{SSR} + \text{SSE} + 2\cdot\text{(cross term)}SST=SSR+SSE+2⋅(cross term)

The identity holds only when the cross term vanishes. Here it does not:

∑(y^−yˉ)⋅r=(−24.2)(+3)+(−12.2)(−1)+(−4.2)(+2)+(18.8)(−4)+(21.8)(0)=−144.0\sum(\hat{y} - \bar{y})\cdot r = (-24.2)(+3) + (-12.2)(-1) + (-4.2)(+2) + (18.8)(-4) + (21.8)(0) = -144.0∑(y^​−yˉ​)⋅r=(−24.2)(+3)+(−12.2)(−1)+(−4.2)(+2)+(18.8)(−4)+(21.8)(0)=−144.0

SSR+SSE+2(−144.0)=1610.80−288.00=1322.80=SST\text{SSR} + \text{SSE} + 2(-144.0) = 1610.80 - 288.00 = 1322.80 = \text{SST}SSR+SSE+2(−144.0)=1610.80−288.00=1322.80=SST

The books are quoting a result about least-squares fits. Least squares forces the residuals to be uncorrelated with the fitted values — that is exactly what setting the gradient to zero achieves. Our ŷ column was invented by hand, so no such guarantee applies.

warning

R2=SSR/SSTR^2 = SSR/SSTR2=SSR/SST and SST=SSR+SSESST = SSR + SSESST=SSR+SSE require predictions from an ordinary least-squares fit with an intercept. R2=1−SSE/SSTR^2 = 1 - SSE/SSTR2=1−SSE/SST requires nothing. Use the second form always: it is what r2_score computes, and the only one still correct for ridge, a tree, a neural network, or a number a colleague typed into a spreadsheet.

danger

r2_score is not symmetric. Swapping the arguments does not flip a sign; it computes 1 − SSE/Σ(ŷ − mean(ŷ))² — right numerator, wrong denominator. The result looks plausible, which is why the bug survives review. mean_squared_error is symmetric, so the habit you built there does not transfer.

The baseline is also computed from the rows being scored, not from training. Score identical predictions on two test splits and you get two values of SST, hence two R² values, from one model — the same split lottery that makes a single train/test split unreliable in the first place.

Adding any feature can only push in-sample R² up​

The defect in one sentence: least squares with p+1 features can always reproduce the best solution found with p features, by setting the new coefficient to zero.

So the minimum achievable SSE cannot rise when a column is added. SST does not move — it depends only on y. Therefore SSE/SST cannot rise and R² = 1 − SSE/SST cannot fall.

ClaimStatus
Adding a useful feature raises in-sample R²True
Adding a useless feature raises in-sample R²Also true — tiny, but never zero and never negative
Adding a column of pure random noise raises in-sample R²Also true
In-sample R² can therefore detect an over-featured modelFalse. It structurally cannot
With p = n − 1 features, in-sample R² is exactly 1True — the fit interpolates every point, SSE = 0

That last row is the reductio: nineteen features on twenty rows gives R² = 1.0 and a model that knows nothing. "R² = 0.99" is not a boast until you say how many features, and whether it was measured in-sample or out-of-sample.

Out-of-sample R² has no such guarantee. Adding a junk feature usually lowers test R², because the coefficient was fitted to noise that does not repeat. Watch the gap between the two.

Adjusted R² charges rent for every feature​

Adjusted R² makes each feature pay for the degree of freedom it consumes. Instead of comparing raw sums of squares it compares variance estimates — each sum divided by its own degrees of freedom:

Radj2=1−SSE/(n−p−1)SST/(n−1)=1−(1−R2)⋅n−1n−p−1R^2_{adj} = 1 - \frac{\text{SSE} / (n - p - 1)}{\text{SST} / (n - 1)} = 1 - (1 - R^2) \cdot \frac{n - 1}{n - p - 1}Radj2​=1−SST/(n−1)SSE/(n−p−1)​=1−(1−R2)⋅n−p−1n−1​

The two forms are one expression rearranged; use the right-hand one, because you almost always have R² already. For the five-house fit: n = 5, p = 1, so n − p − 1 = 3 and n − 1 = 4.

1−R2=1−0.98246145=0.017538551 - R^2 = 1 - 0.98246145 = 0.017538551−R2=1−0.98246145=0.01753855

Radj2=1−0.01753855×(4/3)=1−0.02338473=0.97661527≈0.9766R^2_{adj} = 1 - 0.01753855 \times (4/3) = 1 - 0.02338473 = 0.97661527 \approx 0.9766Radj2​=1−0.01753855×(4/3)=1−0.02338473=0.97661527≈0.9766

R² was 0.9825; Adjusted R² is 0.9766. The gap is one feature's rent, paid out of five rows. Four facts fall straight out of the formula:

FactWhy
R²_adj ≤ R² alwaysThe multiplier (n−1)/(n−p−1) is ≥ 1 whenever p ≥ 0
Equal only when p = 0The multiplier becomes (n−1)/(n−1) = 1. The baseline scores 0 on both

The rent is too cheap to make Adjusted R² a feature selector​

Work out exactly when Adjusted R² rises. Adding one feature improves it if and only if that feature's t-statistic satisfies t² > 1, that is abs(t) > 1.

ThresholdEffective rule
abs(t) > 1Adjusted R² rises — the feature is kept
abs(t) > 2The coefficient is roughly significant at the 5% level
AICCharges 2 per parameter instead of 1 — a strictly stronger penalty

Adjusted R² accepts a feature at half the evidence you would demand before calling its coefficient real. A pure-noise column clears abs(t) > 1 about 32% of the time by chance alone, so throw ten noise columns at a model and roughly three will look like improvements — the multiple-regression page runs that experiment and shows the penalty being outrun.

JobAdjusted R²
Warn you that R² rose only because p roseGood at this
Compare two nested models on the same rowsFine
Compare models fitted to different targets or row countsInvalid — SST differs
Select features from a large poolToo permissive. Cross-validate instead
tip

The reliable version of the same question needs no formula: fit both models, score both on rows neither has seen, prefer the winner there. Adjusted R² is an in-sample approximation of that comparison, invented when refitting a model fifty times was expensive. It no longer is.

The two differ because MAPE is a mean of ratios and MAE/ȳ is a ratio of means. MAPE gives row 1 — the cheapest house — more weight per rupee of error than row 5. That is either the point or a distortion, depending on whether a 5% miss on a cheap house matters as much as on a dear one.

Three ways MAPE misbehaves​

1. Undefined at y=0y = 0y=0, and scikit-learn hides it. A true value of zero divides by zero. scikit-learn does not raise; it substitutes np.finfo(float).eps ≈ 2.22e−16 for the denominator, so one zero-valued row produces a MAPE around 1e+15 and drowns every other row. Nothing warns you.

2. Over-prediction is punished without limit; under-prediction is capped at 100%. Predicting zero is the worst an under-prediction can do. Over-prediction has no ceiling, so a model tuned to minimise MAPE shades its forecasts downward — a documented bias, not a curiosity.

3. Identical MAE, MAPE 100× apart. Two rows, y = [10, 1000], two constant models:

Predict 10[0, 990]495[0%, 99%]49.5%
Predict 1000[990, 0]495[9900%, 0%]4950%

Same MAE. MAPE differs by a factor of 100, entirely because of which row carries the error.

WAPE is MAE rescaled by the mean of y. So if all you wanted was an error you could quote as a percentage, WAPE gets you there without any of MAPE's pathologies — and it is what most demand-planning teams actually report.

3. Common Mistakes​

  • 1. R² bound expectation
  • 2. SSR/SST equivalence
  • 3. In-sample Complexity Guard
  • 4. Scikit-learn's MAPE scale

  • The Misconception: "R² is the square of the correlation coefficient, so it must stay bounded within [0, 1]."
  • Wrong Thinking: Believing a negative R2R^2R2 is mathematically impossible or represents a code crash.
  • The Right Principle: R2=1−SSE/SSTR^2 = 1 - SSE/SSTR2=1−SSE/SST is relative and has no lower bound. A negative R2R^2R2 (e.g. −0.6586-0.6586−0.6586) means your model predicts worse than simply guessing the average yˉ\bar{y}yˉ​ for every single row.

  • The Misconception: "I can compute R² as SSR/SST for any machine learning model."
  • Wrong Thinking: Using SSR/SSTSSR/SSTSSR/SST on neural networks, random forests, or manual spreadsheet predictions.
  • The Right Principle: SST=SSR+SSESST = SSR + SSESST=SSR+SSE is a conditional identity that holds only for OLS fits with an intercept (where the cross term ∑(y^i−yˉ)ri\sum(\hat{y}_i - \bar{y})r_i∑(y^​i​−yˉ​)ri​ is forced to exactly 0). For any other model, using SSR/SSTSSR/SSTSSR/SST can yield impossible answers (like 1.19511.19511.1951). Always use 1−SSE/SST1 - SSE/SST1−SSE/SST.

  • The Misconception: "My in-sample R² rose from 0.82 to 0.835, so my new features are helping the model."
  • Wrong Thinking: Adding features based strictly on training R2R^2R2 improvements.
  • The Right Principle: In-sample R2R^2R2 never decreases when features are added, because the optimizer can always set the new weights to 0. Use Adjusted R2R^2R2 (which dropped 0.812→0.8070.812 \to 0.8070.812→0.807) or, better yet, out-of-sample cross-validation.

  • The Misconception: "Scikit-Learn's MAPE function returns the error as a literal percentage."
  • Wrong Thinking: Printing mean_absolute_percentage_error and reporting an error of 0.0296%.
  • The Right Principle: The function returns a fraction, not a percentage. You must multiply by 100 yourself (e.g., 0.0296 represents 2.96%).

4. Summary​

Core Theory

  • R² (Determination): 1−SSE/SST1 - SSE/SST1−SSE/SST measures the share of baseline mean-guess error removed by the model. Range is (−∞,1](-\infty, 1](−∞,1].
  • Conditional Identity: SST=SSR+SSESST = SSR + SSESST=SSR+SSE holds only when OLS orthogonality cancels out the cross term ∑(y^i−yˉ)ri\sum (\hat{y}_i - \bar{y})r_i∑(y^​i​−yˉ​)ri​.
  • Adjusted R²: Divides sums of squares by degrees of freedom, penalizing the score for each feature added.
  • WAPE (Weighted APE): ∑∣ri∣/∑∣yi∣\sum |r_i| / \sum |y_i|∑∣ri​∣/∑∣yi​∣ is mathematically identical to MAE/yˉMAE/\bar{y}MAE/yˉ​ rescaled by 100, bypassing MAPE's pathologies.

Engineering Realities

  • R² scale-free trap: Because R² is unitless, it is easy to compare blindly without verifying the different SSTSSTSST baselines of other splits.
  • Asymmetric MAPE: Under-prediction is capped at 100%, whereas over-prediction is completely unbounded. Tuning on MAPE biases predictions low.
  • The Zero Trap: If true yi=0y_i = 0yi​=0, scikit-learn's MAPE silently divides by eps ≈ 2.22e-16, yielding artificial 1e+151e+151e+15 values. Use WAPE instead.
  • Cheap Adjusted R²: It keeps any feature with t2>1t^2 > 1t2>1, which pure noise clears 32% of the time. Use AIC or out-of-sample CV instead.

info

Key Takeaways

  • R² Asymmetry: Unlike MSE, r2_score is not symmetric. Swapping arguments computes predictions against the wrong variance denominator, yielding silent review bugs.
  • In-sample limit: With p=n−1p = n - 1p=n−1 features, in-sample R2=1.0000R^2 = 1.0000R2=1.0000 is mathematically guaranteed by exact interpolation, carrying zero generalization value.
  • sMAPE: Symmetric MAPE divides by the mean of true and predicted values, keeping errors safely bounded by 200%.

5. [THEORY] What is WAPE, and why does it bypass all of MAPE's numerical pathologies?

  • The Definition: Weighted Absolute Percentage Error is: WAPE=∑∣yi−y^i∣∑∣yi∣⋅100%\text{WAPE} = \frac{\sum \lvert y_i - \hat{y}_i \rvert}{\sum \lvert y_i \rvert} \cdot 100\%WAPE=∑∣yi​∣∑∣yi​−y^​i​∣​⋅100%
