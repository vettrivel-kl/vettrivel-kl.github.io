# Simple Linear Regression

> **Topic —** The first actual algorithm, and the one worth understanding completely, because almost everything later is a variation on it. This page fits a line to a single feature and works the arithmetic all the way through by hand — two coefficients, five error measures, no estimator doing anything you cannot check on paper.

---

## Regression versus classification

The distinction from [Types of Learning](../01-foundations/03-types-of-learning.mdx#the-rule-worth-memorising), restated because this is where it starts to matter:

| | **Regression** | **Classification** |
|---|---|---|
| Predicts | A **continuous number** | A **category** from a fixed set |
| Target type | Numeric | Categorical |
| Does "close" count? | **Yes** — ₹52 lakh for a ₹50 lakh house is good | No — "cat" for a dog is simply wrong |
| Error measured as | A distance | A count of mistakes |
| Output range | Unbounded | One of $k$ labels |

The load-bearing difference is the third row. Because closeness counts, regression can be scored by *how far off* it was — which is what makes squared error the natural loss, and what makes the whole algebraic solution below possible.

---

## The line, and where its two numbers come from

One feature, one target, a straight line:

$$y = b_0 + b_1 \cdot x$$

| Symbol | Name | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| $b_1$ | **slope** / coefficient | How much $y$ changes per one-unit increase in $x$ |
| $b_0$ | **intercept** | The value of $y$ when $x = 0$ |

Fitting means choosing $b_0$ and $b_1$ to minimize the **sum of squared vertical distances** between each point and the line. That phrase is usually where explanations stop. It shouldn't — the two numbers come out of a short calculation you can do on paper, and doing it once removes most of the mystery from everything that follows.

### The formulas

Minimizing $\sum (y_i - b_0 - b_1 x_i)^2$ — differentiate with respect to each coefficient, set both derivatives to zero, solve — gives exactly this:

$$b_1 = \frac{\sum (x_i - \bar{x})(y_i - \bar{y})}{\sum (x_i - \bar{x})^2} = \frac{S_{xy}}{S_{xx}}$$

$$b_0 = \bar{y} - b_1 \cdot \bar{x}$$

Two things are worth noticing before computing anything.

**$b_1$ is a ratio of sums about the means.** The numerator asks how $x$ and $y$ deviate *together*; the denominator asks how much $x$ deviates at all. If $x$ barely varies, the denominator is tiny and the slope is unstable — which is the same defect that makes a constant column [fail a pre-flight audit](../02-data-preprocessing/01-loading-and-preparing-data.mdx#a-pre-flight-audit).

**$b_0$ is not fitted independently.** Once the slope is known, the intercept is forced: the line must pass through $(\bar{x}, \bar{y})$. Every least-squares line goes through the mean of the data. That is not a convention, it falls out of the algebra.

## How wrong is it? The five error measures

The fit produces predictions, the predictions produce residuals, and every standard error measure is a different summary of those same residuals. Nothing new is needed — they are all computed from the numbers already calculated in the workbench table.

Two aggregates carry everything else:
*   **SSE (Sum of Squared Errors):** $\sum (y_i - \hat{y}_i)^2 = 2.4$ — the quantity the fit minimized.
*   **SST (Total Sum of Squares):** $\sum (y_i - \bar{y})^2 = 6.0$ — the error you would make predicting the mean every time, which is the baseline any model has to beat.

| Measure | Formula | Value | Reads as |
|---|---|---|---|
| **MAE** | $\sum \lvert r_i \rvert / n$ | **`0.6400`** | Average miss, in the units of $y$ |
| **MSE** | $SSE / n$ | **`0.4800`** | Average squared miss — units are $y^2$ |
| **RMSE** | $\sqrt{MSE}$ | **`0.6928`** | Back in the units of $y$, but outlier-sensitive |
| **$R^2$** | $1 - SSE/SST$ | **`0.6000`** | Fraction of variance beaten off the mean baseline |
| **Adjusted $R^2$** | $1 - \frac{(1-R^2)(n-1)}{n-k-1}$ | **`0.4667`** | $R^2$, penalized for using $k$ features |

All four of the first group match scikit-learn's metrics exactly. **Adjusted $R^2$ has no scikit-learn function** — you must compute it yourself, which is worth knowing before you go looking for one.

Three readings that matter more than the definitions:

**$R^2 = 0.6$ means the model beat the mean by 60% of the available variance.** $R^2$ is not a correlation and not an accuracy — it is a comparison against a specific, deliberately simple baseline. A model with $R^2 = 0$ is exactly as good as always guessing $\bar{y}$. A negative $R^2$ is worse than that, and is entirely possible on unseen test data.

**MAE and RMSE differ here ($0.64$ versus $0.69$) and the gap is informative.** $RMSE \ge MAE$ always, with equality only when every residual has the exact same magnitude. The size of the gap is a signal about whether a few large errors dominate the fit.

**Adjusted $R^2$ dropped to $0.4667$ from $0.6$** — a large fall, because with $n = 5$ and $k = 1$ the penalty factor $\frac{n-1}{n-k-1} = 4/3$ is severe. That severity is entirely a function of how small $n$ is relative to $k$, and it is the reason Adjusted $R^2$ behaves so differently on realistic data.

:::note

**Choosing between them is a separate question**

This page computes the five so the arithmetic is never mysterious. *Which* one to report — and the traps in each, including a genuine sign inversion in scikit-learn's cross-validation scoring — belongs with **regression performance metrics**, along with residual diagnostics. Compute first, choose later.

:::

## r, b₁ and R² are three views of one fact

For a one-feature regression with an intercept, the correlation coefficient and the fit are not separate pieces of information. Two exact identities:

$$b_1 = r \times \frac{s_y}{s_x} \qquad \qquad R^2 = r^2$$

On the five default points above, $r = 0.774597$, and both hold to every digit printed:
*   `r × (s_y/s_x) = 0.600000` (equal to $b_1$)
*   `r² = 0.600000` (equal to $R^2$)

The $ddof$ question — population or sample standard deviation — is the one people expect to matter here, and it **does not**: the identity is exact either way because the denominator cancels out of the algebra. One less thing to get wrong.

The first identity says a correlation becomes a slope once you rescale it by the spread of the two variables. The second is narrower than it looks:

:::danger

**$R^2 = r^2$ holds only for simple regression**

With one feature and an intercept, $R^2$ is the squared correlation between $x$ and $y$. With **two or more** features it is not — there is no single $r$ to square, and $R^2$ becomes the squared correlation between $y$ and the model's *predictions* $\hat{y}$. Carrying the $R^2 = r^2$ intuition into multiple regression is a common and quiet error.

:::

## How much data does it take? The 1/√n Law {/* #how-much-data-does-it-take */}

The recovered coefficients are never exactly right, because noise in real-world data is real. The useful question is how fast the estimation error shrinks as we collect more rows. Theory is specific: the standard deviation of the estimated slope is:

$$SD(\hat{b}_1) = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n \cdot Var(x)}}$$

Thus, the error falls as $1/\sqrt{n}$, and $RMSE \times \sqrt{n}$ should be a **constant**. With a true noise $\sigma = 2$ and $x \sim Uniform(0, 10)$, the variance of $x$ is $100 / 12 = 8.333$, making that constant exactly $2 / \sqrt{8.333} = 0.6928$.

### ⚡ 1/√n Law Simulator

Adjust the sliders below to change $n$ (sample size) and $\sigma$ (noise standard deviation). The simulator will instantly run **500 Monte Carlo fits** in your browser to calculate the empirical standard error of the slope and compare it directly to mathematical theory.

export const RootNLawSimulator = () => {
  const [n, setN] = React.useState(200);
  const [sigma, setSigma] = React.useState(2.0);
  const [empSD, setEmpSD] = React.useState(0);
  const [theoSD, setTheoSD] = React.useState(0);

  // Box-Muller transform for normal distribution sampling
  const randNormal = (mean, std) => {
    let u = 0, v = 0;
    while(u === 0) u = Math.random();
    while(v === 0) v = Math.random();
    const num = Math.sqrt(-2.0 * Math.log(u)) * Math.cos(2.0 * Math.PI * v);
    return num * std + mean;
  };

  React.useEffect(() => {
    const trials = 500;
    let b1Estimates = [];
    const varX = 100 / 12; // Variance of Uniform(0, 10)
    
    // Theoretical SD
    const th = sigma / Math.sqrt(n * varX);
    setTheoSD(th);

    // Run Monte Carlo Simulation
    for (let t = 0; t < trials; t++) {
      let sx = 0, sy = 0, sxx = 0, sxy = 0;
      let xs = [];
      let ys = [];
      
      // Generate synthetic points
      for (let i = 0; i < n; i++) {
        const xVal = Math.random() * 10; // Uniform(0,10)
        const yVal = 2.5 * xVal + 7.0 + randNormal(0, sigma);
        xs.push(xVal);
        ys.push(yVal);
      }
      
      const xMean = xs.reduce((a,b) => a+b, 0) / n;
      const yMean = ys.reduce((a,b) => a+b, 0) / n;
      
      for (let i = 0; i < n; i++) {
        sxx += (xs[i] - xMean) ** 2;
        sxy += (xs[i] - xMean) * (ys[i] - yMean);
      }
      
      const b1_fit = sxx === 0 ? 0 : sxy / sxx;
      b1Estimates.push(b1_fit);
    }

    // Calculate Empirical SD (RMSE of b1 - 2.5)
    const rmse = Math.sqrt(b1Estimates.reduce((sum, b) => sum + (b - 2.5) ** 2, 0) / trials);
    setEmpSD(rmse);
  }, [n, sigma]);

  return (
    <div style={{ padding: '20px', border: '1px solid var(--ifm-color-emphasis-200)', borderRadius: '8px', margin: '20px 0', backgroundColor: 'rgba(148, 163, 184, 0.02)' }}>
      <h4 style={{ margin: '0 0 10px 0' }}>🔌 Interactive 1/√n Error Simulator</h4>
      <p style={{ fontSize: '13px', margin: '0 0 15px 0', opacity: 0.9 }}>Run 500 regressions instantly. See how the spread of our slope estimate shrinks as $n$ increases.</p>

      <div style={{ display: 'flex', gap: '15px', flexWrap: 'wrap', marginBottom: '15px' }}>
        <div style={{ flex: '1', minWidth: '150px' }}>
          <label style={{ fontSize: '13px', fontWeight: 'bold', display: 'block', marginBottom: '5px' }}>Sample Size ($n$): <b>{n}</b></label>
          <input type="range" min="20" max="1000" step="20" value={n} onChange={(e) => setN(parseInt(e.target.value))} style={{ width: '100%' }} />
        </div>
        <div style={{ flex: '1', minWidth: '150px' }}>
          <label style={{ fontSize: '13px', fontWeight: 'bold', display: 'block', marginBottom: '5px' }}>Noise Level (σ): <b>{sigma.toFixed(1)}</b></label>
          <input type="range" min="0.5" max="5.0" step="0.5" value={sigma} onChange={(e) => setSigma(parseFloat(e.target.value))} style={{ width: '100%' }} />
        </div>
      </div>

      <div style={{ display: 'grid', gridTemplateColumns: '1fr 1fr', gap: '15px', textAlign: 'center' }}>
        <div style={{ padding: '15px', border: '1px solid var(--ifm-color-emphasis-200)', borderRadius: '6px', backgroundColor: 'rgba(49, 130, 206, 0.03)' }}>
          <div style={{ fontSize: '11px', textTransform: 'uppercase', letterSpacing: '0.5px' }}>Empirical Error SD</div>
          <div style={{ fontSize: '24px', fontWeight: 'bold', color: '#3182ce', margin: '5px 0' }}>{empSD.toFixed(4)}</div>
          <div style={{ fontSize: '11px', opacity: 0.8 }}>Standard error calculated from 500 fits</div>
        </div>
        <div style={{ padding: '15px', border: '1px solid var(--ifm-color-emphasis-200)', borderRadius: '6px', backgroundColor: 'rgba(56, 161, 105, 0.03)' }}>
          <div style={{ fontSize: '11px', textTransform: 'uppercase', letterSpacing: '0.5px' }}>Theoretical Error SD</div>
          <div style={{ fontSize: '24px', fontWeight: 'bold', color: '#38a169', margin: '5px 0' }}>{theoSD.toFixed(4)}</div>
          <div style={{ fontSize: '11px', opacity: 0.8 }}>Calculated as σ / √(n * Var(x))</div>
        </div>
      </div>

      <div style={{ fontSize: '12px', marginTop: '12px', textAlign: 'center', opacity: 0.8 }}>

      </div>

## What "linear" actually restricts

The most common misreading of linear regression is that it can only fit straight lines. It cannot fit *curves in its coefficients* — a completely different restriction.

The data below is a **parabola**, $y = 2x^2 - 3x + 5$ plus noise:

```text title="Output"
5. 'LINEAR' MEANS LINEAR IN THE COEFFICIENTS
   given x            R² = 0.4559   coefficients [-2.8500]
   given x, x²        R² = 0.9826   coefficients [-3.0394  1.9711]
   given x, x², x³    R² = 0.9826   coefficients [-3.1011  1.9705  0.0113]
```

Given only $x$, the model manages $R^2 = 0.4559$ — it is trying to fit a curve with a line. Given $x$ **and** $x^2$ as two columns, it reaches **`0.9826`**, and its coefficients `[-3.0394, 1.9711]` recover the true $-3$ and $2$.

Nothing about the estimator changed. $x^2$ is just another column of numbers, and the model is still computing a weighted sum:

$$y = b_0 + b_1 \cdot x + b_2 \cdot (x^2)$$

which is **linear in $b_0, b_1, b_2$** even though it draws a parabola. Adding $x^3$ gained nothing — $R^2$ is identical at $0.9826$, and the new coefficient came out near zero ($0.0113$).

:::tip

**"Linear" is a statement about the coefficients, not the shape**

A model is linear if the prediction is a **weighted sum of its inputs** — whatever those inputs are.

This is why feature engineering is so powerful here: $x^2, \log(x), x_1 \times x_2$, and $\sin(2\pi h / 24)$ are all just columns, and each extends what a linear model can express without changing the model at all. It is also why adding interaction terms can raise $R^2$ dramatically.

What linear regression genuinely cannot do is *discover* those columns for itself.

:::

## The assumptions

Linear regression is a model of the world, and it assumes four things.

| Assumption | Means | Violated when |
|---|---|---|
| **Linearity** | The relationship really is a weighted sum | Curvature is left in the residuals |
| **Independence** | Rows do not influence each other | Time series; repeated measures per subject |
| **Homoscedasticity** | Error spread is constant across the range | Errors fan out as $y$ grows |
| **Normality of residuals** | Errors are roughly normally distributed | Needed for confidence intervals, not for fitting |

Two are worth stressing. **Independence** is the same requirement that made a random [train/test split](../02-data-preprocessing/06-train-test-split.mdx#2-grouped-rows--the-worst-case) invalid on grouped data — one violation, two consequences. And **normality of residuals** is often over-emphasized: the coefficients are unbiased without it. It matters for inference, not for prediction.

Checking them is residual work — plotting residuals against fitted values and looking for pattern, spread, or curvature — which belongs with **regression performance metrics**.

## Correlation with the target, and why it misleads

The obvious way to decide whether a feature is worth having is to correlate it with the target. Fit each of three features on its own against house price, where the truth is:

$$\text{price} = 3000 \cdot \text{size} - 800 \cdot \text{age} + 50000$$

and `door_colour` is pure noise:

```text title="Output"
6. CORRELATION WITH THE TARGET   (true: price = 3000·size − 800·age + 50000)
   feature            corr   fitted b1  R² alone
   size            +0.9849     3043.86    0.9700
   age             -0.0392     -332.50    0.0015
   door_colour     +0.0419     5160.69    0.0018
```

`size` behaves as expected: correlation $+0.9849$ and $R^2 = 0.9700$. The other two rows are the interesting ones.
*   **`age` has a real effect of $-800$ per year and a marginal correlation of $-0.0392$ (essentially zero).** Its solo $R^2$ is `0.0015`. On this evidence you would drop it!
*   **`door_colour` is meaningless and gets a fitted coefficient of $5160.69$ and solo $R^2$ of `0.0018`** — which is actually *higher* than `age`'s!

### Why the useful feature is invisible

Not a mystery. Price variance is dominated by other terms:
- `size` spread: $150 \text{ units} \times 3000 = 450,000$
- `age` spread: $50 \text{ units} \times 800 = 40,000$
- noise: $\sigma = 20,000$

`age`'s effect is real but roughly a tenth of `size`'s. Correlating `age` against raw `price` asks it to show up against all of that giant `size` variation at once.

    import numpy as np

    X = np.array([1, 2, 3, 4, 5])
    y = np.array([2, 4, 5, 4, 5])

    x_mean, y_mean = X.mean(), y.mean()

    # Sxy = Σ(x_i - x̄)(y_i - ȳ)
    Sxy = np.sum((X - x_mean) * (y - y_mean))
    
    # Sxx = Σ(x_i - x̄)²
    Sxx = np.sum((X - x_mean) ** 2)

    # b1 = Sxy / Sxx
    b1 = Sxy / Sxx
    
    # b0 = ȳ - b1 * x̄
    b0 = y_mean - b1 * x_mean

## 3. Common Mistakes

<Tabs>
  <TabItem value="m1" label="🚨 1. Linear = Straight Lines Only" default>
    <br/>
    
    *   **The Misconception:** *"I thought linear regression could only fit straight lines in 2D space."*
    *   **❌ Wrong Thinking:** Assuming you cannot fit curved data trends like parabolas, exponentials, or sinusoids.
    *   **✅ The Right Principle:** "Linear" refers to being **linear in the coefficients** ($b_0, b_1, b_2$), not the feature shape. By feeding $x^2$ or $\log(x)$ as new columns, the model fits complex curves perfectly while remaining a simple linear estimator.
  </TabItem>
  <TabItem value="m2" label="🚨 2. Independent Intercept">
    <br/>
    
    *   **The Misconception:** *"I thought the intercept $b_0$ is optimized completely independently of the slope $b_1$."*
    *   **❌ Wrong Thinking:** Believing the optimizer picks both parameters freely.
    *   **✅ The Right Principle:** Once the slope $b_1$ is computed, the intercept is locked as $b_0 = \bar{y} - b_1\bar{x}$. Every least-squares regression line must pass through the mean coordinates $(\bar{x}, \bar{y})$.
  </TabItem>
  <TabItem value="m3" label="🚨 3. Marginal Feature Screening">
    <br/>
    
    *   **The Misconception:** *"If a feature has almost zero correlation with the target, it is useless and should be dropped."*
    *   **❌ Wrong Thinking:** Screening out features (like `age` in our housing dataset) based on their solo correlation coefficient with $y$.
    *   **✅ The Right Principle:** High-variance features can completely drown out weaker but important predictors. Always fit features jointly; multiple regression automatically measures each coefficient after holding the others constant.
  </TabItem>
  <TabItem value="m4" label="🚨 4. Adjusted R² function">
    <br/>
    
    *   **The Misconception:** *"I'll just import adjusted_r2_score from sklearn.metrics."*
    *   **❌ Wrong Thinking:** Spending hours looking for a non-existent scikit-learn function.
    *   **✅ The Right Principle:** Scikit-learn has no Adjusted $R^2$ function. You must compute it yourself using $1 - \frac{(1-R^2)(n-1)}{n-k-1}$.
  </TabItem>
</Tabs>

*   **🎯 Convexity:** Squared error is chosen because it is convex and differentiable, leading to a closed-form analytical solution.
*   **⚠️ Outliers:** Squaring the residuals makes the line highly sensitive to outlier values, especially those at high-leverage positions.

### Review Flashcards

<details>
  <summary>❓ <b>1. [THEORY] Why is the intercept $b_0$ not fitted independently of $b_1$?</b></summary>
  <div className="answer-content">
    <br/>
    
    *   **The Derivation:** The least-squares loss is $L = \sum (y_i - b_0 - b_1 x_i)^2$.
    *   Taking the partial derivative with respect to $b_0$ gives:
        $$\frac{\partial L}{\partial b_0} = -2 \sum (y_i - b_0 - b_1 x_i) = 0$$
    *   Dividing by $-2$ and expanding the sum:
        $$\sum y_i - n \cdot b_0 - b_1 \sum x_i = 0$$
    *   Dividing through by $n$:
        $$\bar{y} - b_0 - b_1 \bar{x} = 0 \implies b_0 = \bar{y} - b_1 \bar{x}$$
    *   **The Reality:** This mathematical proof guarantees that the fitted regression line is always forced to pass through the exact mean point $(\bar{x}, \bar{y})$.
  </div>
</details>

<details>
  <summary>❓ <b>2. [ANALYZE] How do outliers far from the mean of $x$ affect the slope compared to outliers near the mean of $x$?</b></summary>
  <div className="answer-content">
    <br/>
    
    *   **Leverage Principle:** The denominator of $b_1$ is $S_{xx} = \sum (x_i - \bar{x})^2$.
    *   The influence (or leverage) of any point on the slope is proportional to its squared distance from the mean of $x$, $(x_i - \bar{x})^2$.
    *   If a point sits exactly at $x_i = \bar{x}$, its $(x_i - \bar{x}) = 0$, meaning it has **zero** leverage over the slope $b_1$ (it only affects the vertical position of the line, i.e., $b_0$).
    *   An outlier far out on the $x$-axis has enormous $(x_i - \bar{x})^2$, acting as a heavy anchor that can single-handedly pivot the entire slope of the line.
  </div>
</details>

<details>
  <summary>❓ <b>3. [THEORY] What baseline does $R^2$ compare the model against, and how can $R^2$ be negative?</b></summary>
  <div className="answer-content">
    <br/>
    
    *   **The Baseline:** $R^2$ compares the model's SSE against the Sum of Squares Total (SST), where $SST = \sum (y_i - \bar{y})^2$. SST represents the error of a "constant baseline model" that always predicts the mean $\bar{y}$.
    *   **Negative $R^2$:** Since $R^2 = 1 - \frac{SSE}{SST}$, it becomes negative when $SSE > SST$.
    *   This occurs when the model's predictions make larger errors than the simple constant mean predictor. This is common when testing a model on unseen out-of-sample data, proving the model is worse than useless.
  </div>
</details>

<details>
  <summary>❓ <b>4. [ANALYZE] If a feature is highly predictive of the target, why might its correlation with the target look near-zero?</b></summary>
  <div className="answer-content">
    <br/>
    
    *   **The Masking Effect:** Correlation measures marginal (one-on-one) relationship. If there is a dominant feature that accounts for 90% of the target's variance, a secondary but highly useful feature's signal is completely buried under that massive leftover variance.
    *   **The Solution:** Subtracting the dominant feature's contribution ("partialing out" the effect) reveals the true, strong correlation of the secondary feature. Multiple regression does this automatically by fitting all coefficients jointly.
  </div>
</details>

<details>
  <summary>❓ <b>5. [THEORY] What does "linear" in linear regression actually restrict? Can it fit a curve?</b></summary>
  <div className="answer-content">
    <br/>
    
    *   **The Restriction:** "Linear" restricts the model to be **linear in its coefficients** ($b_0, b_1, b_2$), not linear in its input shapes.
    *   **Fitting Curves:** It can fit a curve (like a parabola $y = b_0 + b_1 x + b_2 x^2$) because $x^2$ is treated by the estimator as just another column of numeric values. The formula remains a simple weighted sum of inputs.
    *   **The Limitation:** A linear model cannot *discover* these transformations itself; they must be engineered as features manually.
  </div>
</details>
