# Multiple Linear Regression

> **Topic —** The previous page fitted one feature and found something unresolved: `age` had a real effect of $-800$ that its marginal correlation of $-0.0392$ completely hid, and only became visible once `size`'s contribution was removed. Doing that removal automatically, for every feature at once, is what multiple regression *is*. This page works out how, and then asks which measure can be trusted to tell you whether an added feature helped.

---

## The model, and the meaning of one coefficient

Each feature gets its own coefficient:

$$y = b_0 + b_1 x_1 + b_2 x_2 + \dots + b_k x_k$$

With one feature this is a line, with two a plane, with more a **hyperplane** — impossible to picture and no different algebraically.

The definition of $b_i$ is the part that carries all the weight:

> **Holding every other feature in the model constant**, one additional unit of $x_i$ is associated with a $b_i$ increase in $y$.

Both qualifications are load-bearing, and the first one is not a caveat but a description of what the arithmetic actually does. **"Holding every other feature constant"** means $b_i$ is fitted on the variation in $x_i$ that the other features cannot explain — exactly the residual-then-refit operation the previous page did by hand. **"Is associated with"** is not "causes"; regression measures association, and nothing about fitting a hyperplane establishes direction.

---

## Solving for two coefficients by hand

The two-feature case has a closed form small enough to compute on paper, and doing so shows where the "holding constant" behaviour comes from mechanically.

Working in deviations from the means — $d_{1i} = x_{1i} - \bar{x}_1$, and so on — the least-squares coefficients satisfy two simultaneous equations, the **normal equations**:

$$S_{11} \cdot b_1 + S_{12} \cdot b_2 = S_{1y}$$

$$S_{12} \cdot b_1 + S_{22} \cdot b_2 = S_{2y}$$

$$\text{where } S_{11} = \sum d_{1i}^2 \qquad S_{22} = \sum d_{2i}^2 \qquad S_{12} = \sum d_{1i}d_{2i}$$

$$S_{1y} = \sum d_{1i}d_{yi} \qquad S_{2y} = \sum d_{2i}d_{yi}$$

$S_{11}$ and $S_{22}$ measure how much each feature varies. $S_{12}$ measures how much they vary *together* — and it is the only term that couples the two equations. Set $S_{12} = 0$ and they decouple completely: $b_1 = S_{1y}/S_{11}$ and $b_2 = S_{2y}/S_{22}$, each identical to its simple-regression value. **Everything interesting about multiple regression lives in $S_{12}$.**

:::tip

**That determinant is the whole story of collinearity**

`det = S₁₁S₂₂ − S₁₂²` sits in the denominator of both coefficients. $S_{12}$ is the coupling term, so as the two features become more alike, $S_{12}^2$ grows toward $S_{11}S_{22}$ and **the determinant heads to zero** — dividing by something arbitrarily small. This is the algebraic source of every instability later on this page, and at exactly $S_{12}^2 = S_{11}S_{22}$ the determinant is zero and there is no unique solution at all, which is [the next page](./03-the-matrix-formulation.mdx#three-ways-it-breaks)'s subject.

:::

The same structure generalises: with $k$ features you get $k$ simultaneous equations, which is unwieldy by hand and elegant in matrix form — hence the next page.

## What "holding all else constant" does to a coefficient

A coefficient's value depends on **what else is in the model**. Two cases on the house-price data, where the truth is $\text{price} = 3000 \cdot \text{size} - 800 \cdot \text{age} + 50000$:

```text title="Output"
2. WHAT 'HOLDING ALL ELSE CONSTANT' DOES TO A COEFFICIENT
   size's DIRECT effect is 3000 in both models.
   but rooms = 2 + 0.05·size, so in the second model size ALSO acts
   through rooms: 3000 + 5000×0.05 = 3250 is its total marginal effect.

   pair              corr  size alone  size joint    shift
   size, age       0.0614     3043.86     3062.84   +18.98
   size, rooms     0.8244     3218.22     2921.04  -297.18
```

**With a near-orthogonal partner, the coefficient barely moves.** `size` and `age` correlate at $0.0614$, and `size`'s coefficient shifts by $+18.98$ — under 1% — between the solo and joint fits. $S_{12} \approx 0$, so the equations nearly decouple and each feature is estimated as though the other were not there.

**With a correlated partner, it moves substantially and for a specific reason.** `rooms` is built as $2 + 0.05 \cdot \text{size}$, correlating with `size` at $0.8244$. Now `size` influences `price` through two channels: directly at $3000$, and through `rooms` at $5000 \times 0.05 = 250$. Its **total** marginal effect is therefore $3250$, and its **direct** effect is $3000$.

:::danger

**A coefficient is not a property of a feature**

It is a property of a feature **in a particular model**. Add or drop a correlated column and it changes — $3218.22$ to $2921.04$ here. Reporting "size is worth 3000 per unit" without stating what else was in the model is not a rounding-level imprecision; it is an incomplete statement.

:::

## Marginal correlation is a poor screen

The obvious way to choose features is to correlate them with the target. On this data that ranking is:

*   $\text{corr(size, price)} = +0.9849$
*   $\text{corr(age, price)} = -0.0392$
*   $\text{corr(door\_colour, price)} = +0.0419$

`age`, with a real effect of $-800$ per year, ranks **below** `door_colour`, which is pure noise. Any threshold that keeps `door_colour` keeps `age`, and any threshold that drops `age` drops nothing else of value — the screen simply carries no information about these two.

Now fit them in combination:

| Features | $k$ | Train $R^2$ | CV $R^2$ | Adjusted $R^2$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| size | 1 | `0.9700` | `0.9661` | `0.9698` |
| size, age | 2 | `0.9800` | `0.9773` | `0.9797` |
| size, age, door_colour | 3 | `0.9800` | `0.9766` | `0.9795` |
| size, age + 20 noise | 22 | `0.9838` | `0.9732` | `0.9802` |

Three readings, in increasing order of importance:

**`age` genuinely helps.** CV $R^2$ rises $0.9661 \to 0.9773$ when it joins `size`, despite that marginal correlation of $-0.0392$. The joint fit finds the effect the screen missed: once `size` is accounted for, `age` correlates with what remains at $-0.5758$.

**`door_colour` is harmless in small numbers.** Train $R^2$ unchanged at $0.9800$, CV $R^2$ down a hair to $0.9766$. One irrelevant column costs almost nothing.

**Twenty noise columns raised train $R^2$ to $0.9838$ while CV $R^2$ fell to $0.9732$.** Both numbers moved, in opposite directions, from the same change.

:::danger

**Training $R^2$ never decreases when you add a feature**

Not "rarely" — **never**, as a mathematical fact. The model can always set a new coefficient to zero and do exactly as well as before, so the best achievable fit is at least as good as it was. Twenty columns of noise bought $+0.0038$ of training $R^2$ and cost $-0.0041$ of measured out-of-sample performance.

So training $R^2$ cannot be used to decide whether a feature belongs.

:::

The standard answer to that problem is Adjusted $R^2$, which penalises the model for the number of features it uses. Look at its column above: $0.9797$ for `size, age`, and **`0.9802`** for the same model plus twenty columns of pure noise. It ranks the noise model **higher**.

### Why the penalty fails

Adjusted $R^2$ is:

$$\text{adj } R^2 = 1 - (1 - R^2) \times \frac{n - 1}{n - k - 1}$$

and the mechanism is a race between two quantities that happen to grow at similar rates. As $k$ rises, the penalty factor $\frac{n-1}{n-k-1}$ grows — at $k = 102, n = 120$ it is $119/17 = 7.0$, a sevenfold inflation of the unexplained variance. But $(1 - R^2)$ is *shrinking* at almost exactly the rate that offsets it, because spurious columns inflate train $R^2$: from $0.9782$ to $0.9971$, so $(1 - R^2)$ goes $0.0218 \to 0.0029$, a factor of **7.5**.

Multiply the two and you get roughly what you started with. The penalty is real, correctly implemented, and almost exactly cancelled by the overfitting it is meant to expose.

:::danger

**Adjusted R² is not an overfitting guard**

At $n = 120$ it failed to flag 100 columns of pure noise. It is a mild correction for the fact that $R^2$ cannot decrease — useful for comparing two models that differ by a feature or two, and no substitute for measuring out-of-sample performance.

The number that worked here is **cross-validated $R^2$**, which fell monotonically and by $0.3847$. When train $R^2$ and CV $R^2$ diverge, the training number is the one lying — and Adjusted $R^2$ is close enough to a training number to lie with it.

:::

This is also why the honest form of the earlier claim matters: Adjusted $R^2$ and cross-validation are often presented as two interchangeable remedies for feature-count inflation. On this data they are not remotely interchangeable. Only one of them worked.

## When coefficients stop meaning anything

The determinant warning from the hand-worked fit, measured. Two features, each with a true coefficient of exactly $3.00$, fitted 40 times at increasing correlation between them:

| $\text{corr}(x_1, x_2)$ | Mean $coef_1$ | Mean $coef_2$ | Sum | SD of $coef_1$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| `0.000` | `2.9927` | `3.0017` | `5.9944` | **`0.0646`** |
| `0.500` | `2.9918` | `3.0019` | `5.9937` | **`0.0718`** |
| `0.900` | `2.9893` | `3.0038` | `5.9931` | **`0.1426`** |
| `0.990` | `2.9810` | `3.0118` | `5.9928` | **`0.4500`** |
| `0.999` | `2.9554` | `3.0373` | `5.9928` | **`1.4312`** |

Read the columns carefully, because the result is subtle:

*   **The means stay correct.** $2.9554$ and $3.0373$ at the extreme — still centred near $3.00$. Collinearity does **not** bias the coefficients.
*   **The sum stays exact.** $5.9928$ in the last three rows, against a true total of $6.00$, and it barely moves across the whole table.
*   **The spread explodes.** Standard deviation of the first coefficient goes $0.0646 \to 1.4312$ — **22× larger** — and most of that happens between $0.99$ and $0.999$.

The interpretation is precise and worth stating carefully: when two features carry nearly the same information, the data determines their **combined** effect perfectly well and says almost nothing about how to **divide** it between them. Any individual fit picks a split more or less arbitrarily, and the next sample picks a different one.

This is **multicollinearity**. Its consequences:

| Affected | How |
|---|---|
| Predictions | **Barely** — the sum is what drives them, and the sum is stable |
| Individual coefficients | **Severely** — unstable, and can even flip sign |
| Interpretation | **Fatally** — "size adds 3000" is not a supportable claim |
| Standard errors | Inflated, so significance tests fail to reject |

### What ridge does about it

Ridge regression adds a penalty on the size of the coefficients, which resolves the ambiguity by preferring the split that keeps them small. At $\text{corr} = 0.999$:

| Model | Mean $coef_1$ | Mean $coef_2$ | SD of $coef_1$ | Sum | Test $R^2$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| LinearRegression | `3.2657` | `2.7314` | `1.8311` | `5.9971` | `0.9710` |
| Ridge(alpha=1.0) | `3.0206` | `2.9563` | `0.2378` | `5.9769` | `0.9713` |

**The stability gain is dramatic.** Coefficient spread drops $1.8311 \to 0.2378$, an **87% reduction**, and the split becomes even: the gap between the two means narrows from $0.5342$ to $0.0644$.

**The prediction gain is nil.** Test $R^2$ goes $0.9710 \to 0.9713$ — an improvement of **`0.0003`**, which is nothing.

That pairing is the lesson, and it confirms the thesis of the table above from the opposite direction: since collinearity was never damaging the predictions, fixing collinearity cannot improve them. What ridge buys is **coefficients you can report and reproduce**. Whether that is worth a small bias depends entirely on whether anyone is going to read the coefficients.

:::note

**Regularisation gets its own treatment later**

Ridge appears here only as the direct answer to collinearity. The penalty term, choosing its strength, and the different behaviour of **lasso** — which drives coefficients to exactly zero rather than merely shrinking them — belong with **generalisation and model control**.

:::

## 3. Common Mistakes

<Tabs>
  <TabItem value="m1" label="1. Absolute Coefficients" default>
    <br/>
    
    *   **The Misconception:** *"A coefficient is an absolute property of a feature."*
    *   **Wrong Thinking:** Believing `size` is always worth 3000 per unit, regardless of what other columns (like `rooms`) are included in the fit.
    *   **The Right Principle:** A coefficient is a property of a feature **in a particular model context**. Adding or dropping correlated columns immediately shifts other weights (e.g., $3218.22 \to 2921.04$ once `rooms` joined) as credit is shared.
  </TabItem>
  <TabItem value="m2" label="2. Solo Correlation Screening">
    <br/>
    
    *   **The Misconception:** *"I should drop features that have near-zero correlation with the target."*
    *   **Wrong Thinking:** Removing `age` because its solo correlation with house price is $-0.0392$.
    *   **The Right Principle:** Dominant features can completely mask secondary signals. Once dominant features (like `size`) are controlled for, hidden correlations become extremely clear (e.g., `age` partial correlation was $-0.5758$, significantly boosting CV $R^2$).
  </TabItem>
  <TabItem value="m3" label="3. Trusting Adjusted R²">
    <br/>
    
    *   **The Misconception:** *"Adjusted R² is a robust guard against overfitting."*
    *   **Wrong Thinking:** Adding 100 noise columns because Adjusted $R^2$ read $0.9794$ (higher than the clean 2-feature baseline of $0.9779$).
    *   **The Right Principle:** Adjusted $R^2$ is not an overfitting guard. Spurious columns inflate training $R^2$ so rapidly that the inflation nearly cancels out the feature-count penalty. Always trust out-of-sample Cross-Validation scoring.
  </TabItem>
  <TabItem value="m4" label="4. Collinearity biases fits">
    <br/>
    
    *   **The Misconception:** *"Multicollinearity introduces a bias into our regression coefficients."*
    *   **Wrong Thinking:** Believing collinearity makes the averages of fitted coefficients drift away from the true values.
    *   **The Right Principle:** Collinearity does **not** bias coefficients—their means stay exactly correct. Instead, it makes coefficient **variance explode**, creating massive instability where individual weights swing wildly.
  </TabItem>
</Tabs>

**Key Takeaways**

*   **Sum Invariance:** Under multicollinearity, while individual coefficients are unstable and change wildly, their **combined sum remains perfectly stable** and predictions remain robust.
*   **Interpretation vs. Prediction:** Collinearity completely destroys model **interpretation** ("how much size adds"), but has almost no effect on **prediction** performance.
*   **Ridge trade-off:** Ridge regression does not improve accuracy (test $R^2$ stays flat); it buys reportable, stable, and reproducible coefficients.

### Review Flashcards

<details>
  <summary><b>1. [THEORY] What does $S_{12} = 0$ mean, and what happens to the simultaneous normal equations?</b></summary>
  <div className="answer-content">
    <br/>
    
    *   **The Covariance Rule:** $S_{12} = \sum d_{1i} d_{2i}$ measures the covariance (shared variation) between features $x_1$ and $x_2$.
    *   If $S_{12} = 0$, the features are completely orthogonal (uncorrelated).
    *   Substituting $S_{12} = 0$ into the simultaneous normal equations:
        $$S_{11} b_1 + 0 = S_{1y} \implies b_1 = \frac{S_{1y}}{S_{11}}$$
        $$0 + S_{22} b_2 = S_{2y} \implies b_2 = \frac{S_{2y}}{S_{22}}$$
    *   The equations **decouple completely**. Multiple linear regression collapses into two completely independent simple linear regressions, with both slopes equal to their solo values.
  </div>
</details>

<details>
  <summary><b>2. [ANALYZE] How does the determinant $S_{11}S_{22} - S_{12}^2$ head to zero, and why does this explode coefficient variance?</b></summary>
  <div className="answer-content">
    <br/>
    
    *   **The Math:** Solve the normal equations using Cramer's rule. The determinant sits in the denominator of both coefficients $b_1$ and $b_2$:
        $$b_1 = \frac{S_{1y}S_{22} - S_{12}S_{2y}}{S_{11}S_{22} - S_{12}^2}$$
    *   As features become more collinear, their covariance $S_{12}^2$ increases, heading towards the maximum limit $S_{11}S_{22}$ (where $r = 1.0$).
    *   This forces the denominator (determinant) closer and closer to $0$. Dividing by an arbitrarily tiny number makes the calculated coefficient value extremely sensitive to minor fluctuations in target measurements ($S_{1y}, S_{2y}$), causing coefficient variance to explode.
  </div>
</details>

<details>
  <summary><b>3. [THEORY] Why is it mathematically impossible for training $R^2$ to decrease when you add a feature?</b></summary>
  <div className="answer-content">
    <br/>
    
    *   **The Optimization Rule:** OLS operates by finding coefficients $b_j$ that minimize the Sum of Squared Errors (SSE):
        $$\text{Minimize } \sum (y_i - b_0 - b_1 x_{1i} - \dots - b_k x_{ki})^2$$
    *   If you add a new feature $x_{k+1}$, the optimizer has a new parameter $b_{k+1}$ to tune.
    *   In the worst-case scenario where the new feature is completely useless noise, the optimizer can simply assign $b_{k+1} = 0$, achieving the exact same SSE (and thus the exact same $R^2$) as before.
    *   Because the search space of the optimization has expanded, the minimum SSE can only stay the same or decrease, meaning training $R^2$ **can never decrease**.
  </div>
</details>

<details>
  <summary><b>4. [ANALYZE] Why does Adjusted $R^2$ completely fail as an overfitting guard against multiple noise columns?</b></summary>
  <div className="answer-content">
    <br/>
    
    *   **The Formula:** $\text{adj } R^2 = 1 - (1 - R^2) \times \frac{n-1}{n-k-1}$.
    *   Adding noise columns triggers a literal race between two opposing terms:
        1.  As $k$ increases, the penalty ratio $\frac{n-1}{n-k-1}$ grows (at $k=102, n=120$, it inflates unexplained variance by **7.0×**).
        2.  However, because of overfitting, the training unexplained variance $(1 - R^2)$ shrinks from $0.0218 \to 0.0029$ (a **7.5×** reduction).
    *   **The Result:** The two factors nearly cancel each other out, making Adjusted $R^2$ remain completely flat and completely blind to the fact that the model is fitting pure noise.
  </div>
</details>

<details>
  <summary><b>5. [THEORY] How does Ridge regularisation resolve coefficient instability without improving prediction accuracy?</b></summary>
  <div className="answer-content">
    <br/>
    
    *   **Coefficient Stability:** Ridge regression adds an L2-norm penalty ($\alpha \sum b_j^2$) to the OLS cost function. When features are collinear, infinite splits of the coefficients yield the same prediction. OLS picks a split randomly, but Ridge enforces stability by picking the split that keeps both coefficients small and evenly distributed, cutting standard deviation by over 85%.
    *   **No Prediction Gain:** Because OLS was already finding a stable combined sum, its predictions were already robust. Restricting the coefficients via Ridge does not change this sum—it merely changes the individual allocation, leaving prediction score virtually unchanged ($\approx +0.0003$ gain).
  </div>
</details>
